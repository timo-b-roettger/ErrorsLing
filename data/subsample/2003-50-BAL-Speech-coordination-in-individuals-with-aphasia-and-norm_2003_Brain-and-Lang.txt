Speech coordination in individuals with aphasia and normal speakers
Arpita Bose,* Pascal van Lieshout, and Paula A. Square
Oral Dynamics Laboratory, Department of Speech-Language Pathology, University of Toronto,
500 University Avenue, 10th Floor, Toronto, ON, Canada M5G 1V7

Researchers have long recognized that speech motor control difficulties that occur in aphasic individuals specifically seem to affect the
timing and/or integration of movements of two or more articulators.
These difficulties have been attributed to some form of coordination
deficit and various terms have been used to describe this deficit:
``mistiming between articulators,'' or ``intra- and inter-articulator
temporal and spatial dsycoordination'' (Blumstein, 1998; McNeil &
Kent, 1990). These assumptions regarding articulatory coordination
problems in aphasic individuals are based upon a small number of
kinematic studies which have utilized single articulator measurements,
or studies, where coordination problems are inferred from acoustic
measures like voice onset time. Clearly, what is lacking is a more direct
approach, in which the actual nature and stability of coordinative
actions during speech production can be assessed.
The purpose of this study is to determine if there is a difference in
the nature and stability of coordination in aphasic speakers compared
to normal speakers. It is unique among previous studies in aphasia in
the way it allows us to explore the dynamics of coordination directly at
different levels of speech production. For speech, coordination can be
defined as the ability of two or more articulators (e.g., upper lip and
lower lip) to combine as a functional unit to achieve a common goal
(e.g., bilabial closure) for the production of a specific speech sound
(e.g., bilabial stop). Using Articulatory Phonology (Browman &
Goldstein, 1992) and Task Dynamics (Saltzman & Kelso, 1987) as a
theoretical framework we tested the potential differences in higher level
and lower level types of coordination as measured by a continuous
estimate of relative phase (van Lieshout & Moussa, 2000). To elaborate, a higher level of coordination (inter-gestural) involves the combined movements of articulators to realize a coordinative pattern of
independent gestures (e.g., combined movements of lips + jaw coordinated with the combined movements of tongue + jaw for a VCV
sequence like /api/). At a lower level of coordination (intra-gestural),
individual gestures are realized by the coordinated actions of individual articulators (e.g., upper lip, lower lip, and jaw are coordinated to
realize a bilabial closing/opening gesture).

Methods
Participants
Five individuals with aphasia (AS) and five age and gender matched normal speaking individuals (NS) served as subjects. Participants

Procedures
All participants repeated /api/ and /ipa/ at a normal rate for 12 s in
three trials randomly mixed with other trials not further discussed
here. Position data for articulators were collected using an ElectroMagnetic Midsagittal Articulograph (AG 100), and were processed
using custom-made MATLAB procedures. Average relative phase
values (RP) and average within-trial standard deviations of relative
phase (AvSD) were calculated for each trial to measure the nature and
stability of coordination, respectively. For intra-gestural coordination,
the position, and velocity data of UL and LL (corrected for jaw) were
used. For inter-gestural coordination, the Euclidean distance between
UL and LL (+jaw) signals and the Euclidean distance between tongue
body and jaw signals were used as representative signals for bilabial
closure and tongue body constriction gestures, respectively. Data were
analyzed using a mixed ANOVA with repeated measures with
GROUP as between-subject and STIMULI as within-subject factor.

Results and discussion
Table 1 shows the means and SD for average relative phase (RP)
and average of within-trial standard deviation of relative phase (AvSD,
stability index) for AS and NS across two types of coordination.
Average RP did not differ significantly between AS and NS for
either types of coordination. AvSD (stability of coordination) in intragestural coordination showed a significant main effect for STIMULI [F
(1,1) = 43.31, p < .0003] as well as an interaction for GROUP and
STIMULI [F (1,8) = 61.67, p < .0001]. The latter is based on the fact
that AS were more variable for /ipa/ but not for /api/ (see Table 1).
AvSD in inter-gestural coordination showed a significant main effect
for GROUP [F (1,8) = 22.55, p < .0001], with AS showing almost
twice the variability of NS.
These results provide preliminary evidence that in error free fluent
speech AS can coordinate their articulators to produce goal directed
behavior similar to NS (as evidenced by similar RP values). However,
AS significantly differ in the stability of coordination (AvSD) from NS.
At a lower level (intra-gestural) of coordination, the group by stimuli
interaction indicates that larger variability in AS for lip coordination is
limited to (/ipa/), for reasons yet unknown. On the other hand, a strong
overall group difference was evident for higher level (inter-gestural) of
