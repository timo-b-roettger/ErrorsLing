A Comparison of Language
Abilities in Adolescents With Down
Syndrome and Children With
Specific Language Impairment
Glynis Laws
Dorothy V. M. Bishop
University of Oxford,
Oxford, U.K.

This article compared the language profiles of adolescents with Down syndrome
(DS) and children with specific language impairment matched for nonverbal
cognitive ability, and investigated whether similar relationships could be established between language measures and other capacities in both groups. Language profiles were very similar: Expressive language was more affected than
language comprehension, and grammar was more affected than vocabulary in
both domains. Both groups were impaired on tests of grammatical morphology
and phonological memory. There were some differences between the groups, but
these could be attributed to other features of development of people with DS.
KEY WORDS: Down syndrome, specific language impairment, language
disorders

R

esearch on language impairment in Down syndrome (DS) and specific language impairment (SLI) has generally followed separate
streams, but a comparison of the two literatures suggests that
the language profiles of people with DS resemble those of children with
typical SLI (Laws & Bishop, 2003). In both cases, the main weaknesses
are associated with phonology and grammar. This raises the question of
whether the similarities between language profiles are superficial or
whether common underlying mechanisms could be implicated. Our study
approached this question by comparing the language profiles, aspects of
grammatical morphology, and phonological memory of adolescents with
DS with those of younger children with SLI, matched for nonverbal cognitive ability.

Down Syndrome
Cognitive development is compromised in DS; individuals have
moderate to severe levels of intellectual disability. Intriguingly, language ability in DS does not appear to be a simple function of nonverbal IQ. Several cases of exceptional language ability in DS have been
documented, but these do not have concomitantly high levels of general cognitive ability, and there may be little correlation between formal aspects of language and nonverbal cognitive ability in DS (Rondal,
1995).

Similarly, there is no strong evidence that language
impairment is a consequence of the hearing loss reported
for 40-80% of individuals (see Davies, 1996). This is
normally mild to moderate conductive loss, associated
with frequent periods of otitis media, but can include
sensorineural losses by late adolescence. Most research
has either failed to establish a relationship between
hearing loss and language development (e.g., Jarrold &
Baddeley, 1997; Marcell, 1995) or found it to account
for a small proportion (4% to 7%) of the variance in
expressive morphosyntax (Chapman, Schwartz, & KayRaining Bird, 1991; Chapman, Seung, Schwartz, & KayRaining Bird, 2000). Other research has found a relationship between hearing threshold and vocabulary
(Laws & Gunn, in press). Despite inconsistent evidence,
the possible effect of hearing loss on language development of individuals with DS should be taken into account.

Specific Language Impairment
A diagnosis of SLI is applied to children developing
otherwise normally but with language difficulties that
cannot be explained by psychiatric or neurological conditions, by sensory loss, or by inadequate language environments (see Bishop, 1997; Leonard, 1998). SLI is
not a homogeneous condition and a number of subtypes
of the disorder have been identified (e.g., Conti-Ramsden,
Crutchley, & Botting, 1997), but the major subgroup
includes children whose main difficulties are learning
phonology (i.e., the sound system of the language) and
acquiring the rules of grammar.
The nature of the deficit underlying SLI is a matter
of debate (see Bishop, 1992, 1997; Leonard, 1998). On
the basis that language is selectively impaired, some
researchers have attributed deficits to impairment of
the specialized language-learning module described by
Fodor (1983) and Pinker (1994). The main focus of these
researchers was the linguistic content of children's language output (e.g., Rice & Wexler, 1996; van der Lely &
Ullman, 2001). In this conceptualization, deficiencies in
the grammatical structure of language are directly attributed to some fault in innate programs for the initiation and development of human language. Other researchers have been more inclined to theories that
attribute poor language to impairment of one or more
processes or capacities that underpin normal language
development, such as higher level auditory processing
(e.g., Tallal & Piercy, 1973), or phonological memory (e.g.,
Gathercole & Baddeley, 1990). These features could contribute to more general difficulties in acquiring phonetically weak, and thus less salient, grammatical forms
(e.g., Chiat, 2001; Leonard, 1989).
Given the heterogeneous nature of SLI, there is unlikely to be one causal mechanism for the development

of SLI. Also, different components of language may be
differentially affected by any underlying deficits. In a
modular account of language, the system consists of
partially independent components, each with separate
neurobiological substrates (e.g., Tager-Flusberg, 1997).
A computational component is responsible for the linear and hierarchical processing associated with phonology and syntax. A cognitive or conceptual component is
responsible for lexical and semantic aspects of language,
and a social/pragmatic component is required for the
communicative function of language. Finding similar
patterns of strengths and weakness across the language
system in DS and in SLI might suggest that the same
components are affected in both disorders. Review of
the separate literatures would suggest that this is the
case (Laws & Bishop, 2003). Nevertheless, there are
several features of the language profile that are characteristically observed in SLI: (a) language production
is more affected than language comprehension, (b)
grammar is more impaired than vocabulary, (c) certain
aspects of grammatical morphology are disproportionately difficult, and (d) verbal short-term memory is
impaired. A question of interest is whether this pattern of strengths and weaknesses reflects common origins for impairments of expressive syntax and verbal
short-term memory or whether these are instead cooccurring impairments that can be dissociated in other
populations. DS is interesting in this regard, because
it is also characterized by deficits in verbal short-term
memory, and grammatical difficulties have been noted.
However, there is less research on DS than on SLI, and
so it remains uncertain whether the detailed profile of
language skills is really similar in the two disorders or
whether the resemblance is merely superficial. We now
briefly review what is known about the language profile in SLI and DS, before describing our own study,
which was designed to make more explicit comparisons
between the two disorders.

Language Characteristics of DS
and SLI
Comprehension Versus Production
In SLI, it is traditional to distinguish between children with purely expressive language impairment and
those with combined receptive-expressive impairment.
However, Bishop (1979) found measurable comprehension problems even for children categorized as having
pure expressive language impairment, though these
were typically much less severe than the expressive difficulties. This pattern of deficits is evident in the samples
of many studies (e.g., McArthur & Hogben, 2001; Montgomery, 2000). Because DS is recognized from birth, the
early divergence of expressive language abilities from

This gap between language production and comprehension continues to grow throughout childhood and adolescence (Chapman et al., 1991; Chapman, Seung,
Schwartz, & Kay-Raining Bird, 1998), and eventually

abilities (e.g., Laws, 1998; Rosin, Swift, Bless, & Vetter,

Vocabulary Versus Grammar
A dissociation between vocabulary and grammar
acquisition has been established in SLI (e.g., Aram,
Ekelman, & Nation, 1984; Gopnik, 1990; Stark et al.,
1984; Tomblin & Zhang, 1999) and in DS (e.g., Byrne,
Buckley, MacDonald, & Bird, 1995; Chapman, 1995;
Chapman et al., 1991; Chapman, Ross, & Seung, 1993;
Fowler, 1990, 1995; Laws, 1998; Laws & Gunn, in press;
Miller, 1987, 1988; Rondal, 1995; Rosin et al., 1988). In
both populations, morphology and syntax are more affected than vocabulary. In SLI, expressive syntax is an
important predictor of later language ability (Bishop &
Edmundson, 1987; Botting, Faraghar, Simkin, Knox, &
Conti-Ramsden, 2001; Davison & Howlin, 1997). In DS,
an early ceiling in grammatical competence may limit
development, although there is some debate about
whether this is so (Chapman et al., 1998; Thordardottir,
Chapman, & Wagner, 2002). There is clearer evidence
that vocabulary continues to grow beyond that predicted
by nonverbal abilities and often exceeds that of nondisabled children of comparable MA or MLU (Barrett
& Diniz, 1989). There is a different picture for the acquisition of syntactic structure, which is poorer than
would be predicted from nonverbal cognitive abilities
(e.g., Chapman, 1995; Chapman et al., 1998; Fowler,
1990, 1995) or relative to the performance of individuals with other genetic syndromes (e.g., Abbeduto et al.,
2001).

Specific Aspects of Grammatical
Morphology
Poor acquisition of grammatical morphology may
be a reliable clinical marker for SLI and reflect the
failure of linguistic processes to develop normally (Rice
& Wexler, 1996; Rice, Wexler, & Hershberger, 1998; van
der Lely & Ullman, 2001). Many children with SLI consistently find specific grammatical morphemes difficult
(Rice & Wexler, 1996). These tend to be the forms that
typically developing children are slowest to adopt, resulting in a period of "optional" use of correct finite
verb endings. In SLI, this period is longer, leading Rice
1326

and colleagues to refer to an "extended optional infinitive" stage, which they attributed to a deficit in genetic
factors that determine grammatical competence. In particular, children with SLI have difficulties in acquiring verb tense markers, which may persist up to 8 years
of age (Rice et al., 1998). Problems with low-level auditory perception do not appear to account for this deficit: Mild to moderate hearing impairments in children
do not usually hinder the acquisition of finite verb
morphology (Norbury, Bishop, & Briscoe, 2001) or syntactic comprehension (Norbury, Bishop, & Briscoe,
2002).
Morphological deficits have also been described in
DS (Chapman et al., 1998; Eadie, Fey, Douglas, & Parsons, 2002; Rutter & Buckley, 1994). Eadie et al. directly
compared the use of a range of morphemes by children
with DS or SLI matched for mean length of utterance
(MLU; 3.5 to 4.5 morphemes). Verb tense markers (regular and irregular past tense, third-person singular,
copular and auxiliary BE, and modals) were studied, as
well as various nontense morphemes (plurals, -ing, articles, and possessive inflections). There were no statistically significant differences between the SLI and DS
groups for tense or nontense composite variables. However, an investigation of individual morphemes showed
that the DS group were relatively good at using irregular past and third-person irregular tense forms, while
the SLI group were relatively good at using regular past
tense forms.

Phonological Short-Term
Memory
There is considerable research evidence for the importance of phonological memory in the normal development of language (Baddeley, Gathercole, & Papagno,
1998; Gathercole & Baddeley, 1993). Phonological
memory has been measured by nonword repetition, a
task which requires the repetition of unfamiliar but
wordlike sequences of phonemes such as prindle and
penneriful (Gathercole, Willis, Baddeley, & Emslie,
1994). Children with SLI find this task difficult, especially repetition of longer nonword stimuli (e.g., Bishop,
North, & Donlan, 1996; Dollaghan & Campbell, 1998;
Edwards & Lahey, 1998; Ellis Weismer et al., 2000;
Gathercole & Baddeley, 1990). Although phonological
memory difficulties could be due to generally poor language skills (Snowling, Chiat, & Hulme, 1991; van der
Lely & Howard, 1993), poor nonword repetition can be
found in cases of resolved SLI, even after other measured language skills have normalized (Bishop et al.,
1996). Poor phonological memory is also a feature of DS
(see Jarrold, Baddeley, & Phillips, 1999) and may be a
significant predictor of language development (Laws,
1998; Laws & Gunn, in press).

Overview of Design
The evident similarities between the two populations leads to the question of whether the language impairment associated with DS could have the same aetiology as SLI (see Laws & Bishop, 2003). As a first step,
this study directly compared the pattern of strengths and
weaknesses across the language domain for adolescents
with DS to a sample of younger children with SLI and
to a comparison group of typically developing children
at the same nonverbal mental age. The aim was to investigate whether direct comparison of language profiles confirms the similarities in the language impairment of the two populations presented by the separate
literatures and to identify potential differences between
them. We were further interested in whether the language impairments in both groups could be associated
with either a linguistic deficit or with phonological
memory deficits. One important difference between the
two populations is the prevalence of hearing loss in the
DS population. Hearing thresholds were measured in
these participants so that any contribution that hearing loss might make to language impairment in DS could
be accounted for; other participants were screened for
hearing difficulties.

Method
Participants
Down Syndrome
Nineteen participants with DS, 12 females and 7
males, took part in the study. Sixteen of them, between
the ages of 10 and 19 years, were selected from a group
involved in a previous study (Laws & Gunn, in press).
To avoid significant test floor effects, only individuals
with receptive vocabulary equivalent to 4 years or more
on the British Picture Vocabulary Scale-Second Edition
(BPVS-II; Dunn, Dunn, Whetton, & Burley, 1997) were
included. Individuals with hearing aids were excluded,
as were participants over 20 years of age, due to the
decline of language and memory performance of some
young adults over 5 years of study. To increase sample
size, 3 additional participants were recruited. In 6 cases,
parents confirmed a diagnosis of trisomy 21; for other
cases, karyotypes were either not known or questionnaires
were not returned. All participants attended schools for
children with severe learning difficulties (SLD)1 or colleges of further education. The hearing of all but 3 participants with DS was tested using a pure-tone audiometer (Micro Audiometrics Corp., Murphy, NC). Testing took
place in the same rooms used for language assessments.
1

In the United Kingdom, the term learning difficulty is used to refer to
children with nonspecific developmental delay, in place of the previous
term mental handicap.

Individuals were tested at 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000
Hz. The average threshold across both ears and all tested
frequencies was 32.17 dB (range = 21.25-38.75 dB).

Specific Language Impairment
The SLI sample initially included 19 children; 17
from language units attached to mainstream schools and
2 from a special school for language impaired children.
Language units in the United Kingdom include children
with a range of diagnoses and levels of nonverbal ability. Given our research question, it would have been problematic to select children who conformed to the language
profile already established for the DS sample. To avoid
this, we asked teachers or speech and language therapists to identify children between the ages of 4 and 7
years who had language impairments but no additional
neurological or psychiatric diagnoses (e.g., autism), no
physical or sensory impairments, and no severe behavior problems. The nature of the language difficulties we
were interested in was not specified in greater detail.
Nonverbal ability was assessed using Raven's Progressive Coloured Matrices (Raven, Court, & Raven, 1986);
those providing a suitable match to one of the DS sample
were selected for further testing. We aimed to individually match SLI and DS participants on raw Raven's
scores, and achieved this within -2 to +3 points. Twelve
children were also matched for gender. Children with
SLI remained in the sample only if they scored more
than 1 SD below the mean on at least two language tests.
Application of this criterion for impairment led to the
exclusion of 3 children with SLI. This was not necessarily surprising, because we would expect the difficulties
of some children admitted to a language unit to resolve.
Although not impaired on formal tests, they might nevertheless continue to receive language-learning support
away from the mainstream classroom. One additional
child was tested to replace exclusions, so the final sample
of children with SLI reported was 17 (12 boys and 5 girls).
All passed a hearing screening threshold of 25 dB.

Typically Developing Children
Nineteen typically developing children between the
ages of 4 and 7 years were recruited from mainstream
schools and matched on raw Raven's scores (within 1
point) to individuals with DS. All DS-control pairs were
gender matched. One control child was subsequently
found to meet our criteria for SLI and was excluded.
The entire final control sample of 18 children passed a
hearing screening threshold of 25 dB.

all participants. Testing took place in a reasonably quiet

Assessments
Raven's Coloured Progressive Matrices. This test of
nonverbal reasoning requires the selection of a correct
pattern piece to complete a design. Scores were used to
match samples and as a measure of nonverbal MA in
the analyses.
BPVS-II. This test requires selection of the correct
picture in response to a word spoken by the experimenter.
Test for Reception of Grammar (TROG; Bishop,
1983). This grammar comprehension measure requires
participants to select the correct picture from a choice
of four in response to a phrase or sentence spoken by
the experimenter. Items are grouped in blocks of four,
and a point is awarded if responses to all four items in a
block are correct. For most items, incorrect responses
can be classified as lexical or grammatical errors, providing for an investigation of the reason for children's
difficulties.
Expressive Vocabulary Subtest From the Kaufman
Assessment Battery for Children (K-ABC; Kaufman &
Kaufman, 1983). Participants must supply the correct
name for photographed objects.
Recalling Sentences Subtest From the Clinical Evaluation of Language Fundamentals-Revised (CELF-R;
Semel, Wiig, & Secord, 1987). This test involves recall
of sentences of increasing length and grammatical complexity. Scores are based on the number of errors, with
3 points awarded for an error-free response, 2 points for
a response that includes 1 error, and so on.
MLU. Participants narrated the picture book story
Frog, Where Are You? (Mayer, 1996). The experimenter
slowly turned the pages so that the participant could
get an idea of the story, then returned to the beginning
of the book and asked the participant to tell the story.
Three narratives provided by children with SLI were
not sufficiently intelligible for transcription and so were
excluded from analysis. Narratives were transcribed on
the same day into a word processing file by the experimenter. The first author later checked the transcriptions
against the recordings for agreement. The number of
words and additional morphemes produced and MLU
in words and additional morphemes were calculated by
the experimenter. These measures were subsequently
recalculated by a research assistant and any disagreements were resolved.
Elicitation of Third-Person Singular and Past Tense
Verb Endings. Prepublication versions of two subtests

1328

from the Rice/Wexler Test of Early Grammatical Development were used (Rice & Wexler, 2001). To elicit
third-person singular /s/ verb endings, a set of 12 pictures was shown featuring people engaged in an occupation (e.g., policeman, singer). The examiner said, "Here
is a _____. Tell me what a ____ does".
To elicit past tense verb endings, a set of 19 pairs of
picture cards was used. The first of each pair showed a
boy or girl engaged in some activity; the second picture
showed the activity completed. The examiner said, "Here
the boy/girl is ______. Now he/she is finished. Tell me
what he/she did". Participants' responses were audiorecorded. Scores obtained were (a) number of correct uses
of third-person singular verbs, (b) number of correct uses
of past tense verbs, and (c) numbers of regular and irregular past tense verbs used correctly. Fourteen adolescents with DS and 15 children with SLI were able to
complete both tasks. Although all controls completed the
third-person singular elicitation task, scores for the past
tense task were available only for 11 children due to
equipment failure.
Children's Test of Nonword Repetition (CNRep;
Gathercole & Baddeley, 1996). This test of phonological
short-term memory requires repetition of 40 items consisting of two-, three-, four-, or five-syllable nonwords.
Presentation differed from the standard administration
described in the manual. First, items were grouped by
length. If all items at a particular length were incorrectly
produced, testing ceased; this saved the demotivating effect of administering a lengthy test to participants who
struggled with every item. Second, nonwords were spoken by the experimenter rather than presented on audiotape. To ensure that lip movements could not be used
as cues by participants, the experimenter sat to the side
of and slightly behind the participant. Although these
changes were made to accommodate the needs of the
group with DS, the same procedure was followed for the
other two groups to ensure consistency.
Word Repetition. Participants also repeated 20 words
of one to four syllables. Those of 1-3 syllables (e.g., pot,
rabbit, newspaper) had been selected by Gathercole and
Adams (1993) on the basis of early acquisition (Gilhooly
& Logie, 1980) and high imageability (Paivio, Yuille, &
Madigan, 1968). In addition, four-syllable words likely
to be known to young children were offered (e.g., helicopter, caterpillar, television, cauliflower, alligator).
All responses were recorded on audiotape using
a Panasonic recorder (Model RQ-L30) and a TCM160
tie-clip microphone (AV-Leader, Taiwan, ROC). Responses were scored immediately and, later, rescored
from the tapes. Reliability between these scores was
calculated as .92 for nonwords and .99 for words. The
scores assigned during the assessments were used in
the analyses.

Results
Data were analyzed using SPSS Version 11.0 for
Windows. Except where stated otherwise, p < .05 was
accepted for statistical significance. There were no significant correlations between the hearing thresholds of
adolescents with DS and most principal measures, so
most analyses did not take hearing differences into account. The gender distribution of the SLI sample was
different from that of the other groups (DS: 7 males, 12
females; SLI: 12 males, 5 females; controls: 7 males, 12
females), but there were no sex differences in performance on any of the principal measures in any group.
In a study of this nature, it would be usual to convert raw scores to standard scores based on the norms
for chronological age (CA) provided in test manuals.
However, this would be relatively uninformative, because standard scores of most individuals with DS
would be close to the test floor. Thus, standardized
scores would simply confirm that individuals with DS
function at a lower level, without addressing the issue
of whether the pattern of language function is in line
with overall mental development. Accordingly, we converted raw scores to standard scores using test norms,
but indexing these by Raven's age equivalent scores
(MA) rather than CA (MA-indexed standard scores).

scores for all three groups. The ranges of Raven's age

Language Profiles
Differences among the groups' language test scores
were statistically tested using one-way ANOVA with post
hoc Scheffe tests applied to establish the locus of significant group effects. First, we considered the two receptive tests. Grammar understanding (TROG) was
equally impaired in both groups, but receptive vocabulary (BPVS-II) was more problematic for the children
with SLI than for the DS group. However, insofar as
mean receptive vocabulary scores for DS and SLI failed
to meet the criterion for impairment, both groups could
be said to demonstrate similar profiles of language comprehension, that is, grammar understanding is impaired
but receptive vocabulary is unimpaired.
A similar pattern emerged for expressive language.
On both sentence recall and MLU, the DS and SLI
groups were impaired relative to control children but
did not differ from each other. For expressive vocabulary, the pattern of differences resembled that seen for
receptive vocabulary: SLI children had significantly
lower scores than controls but did not differ from the
group with DS. The group with DS did not differ from
controls on expressive vocabulary.
To compare the relative size of deficits in language
comprehension and language expression, composite
scores for each domain were calculated. Raw scores for
the five language tests were converted to z scores, relative to controls, and used to calculate mean receptive z
scores (based on TROG and BPVS-II) and mean expressive z scores (based on CELF-R Sentence Repetition,
Expressive Vocabulary, and MLU). Means indicated
lower levels of expressive language in the SLI and DS
groups relative to levels of language comprehension:
mean expressive SLI = -2.66, SD = 1.24; mean receptive SLI = -1.21, SD = 0.92; mean expressive DS = -2.74,
SD = 1.84; mean receptive DS = -0.88, SD = 1.18. A
repeated-measures ANOVA of composite z scores confirmed a significant effect of domain, F(1, 31) = 71.83,
p < .0001, 2 = .70, but no effect of group, F(1, 31) < 1,
and no interaction, F(1, 31) = 1. Composite scores of expressive language were significantly lower than those
of comprehension for both the impaired groups.
A similar approach was taken to compare grammar
and vocabulary. Mean grammar z scores (based on
TROG, Sentence Repetition, and MLU) and mean vocabulary z scores (based on BPVS-II and Expressive
Vocabulary) were calculated. Means indicated lower

levels of grammar relative to vocabulary in both impaired groups (SLI mean grammar = -2.19, SD = 1.15;
SLI mean vocabulary = -1.89, SD = 1.32; DS mean grammar = -2.65, SD = 1.34; DS mean vocabulary = -1.00,
SD = 1.97). A repeated-measures ANOVA confirmed a
significant effect of domain, F(1, 31) = 16.95, p < .0001,
2 = .35, and no difference between the SLI and DS

.01, 2 = .21, reflecting the slightly wider discrepancy
between grammar and vocabulary scores in the DS

Error Analysis of TROG Scores
Further investigation of the deficit in grammar comprehension was possible with an analysis of errors made
in response to the TROG. The language-impaired groups
provided similar numbers of incorrect responses to each
test block. The error analysis was used to confirm that,
for both groups, poor test performance could be associated with grammatical difficulties rather than low vocabulary or because the child did not remember the sentence or did not attend properly. Responses to items in
TROG Blocks D to L can be categorized as lexical errors
(where the distractor depicts a different noun, adjective,
or verb than the target sentence) or grammatical errors
(where the distractor depicts a situation that could be
described by the same words as the target sentence but
in a different grammatical construction). For items in
Blocks D and F, only lexical errors are possible. For each
of the other blocks (i.e., 28 items in all), the distractors
include two pictures that indicate lexical errors and one
that indicates a grammatical error. By chance alone,
more lexical than grammatical errors are likely, but
there were few lexical errors in any group (DS: M = 0.63,
SD = 1.16; SLI: M = 0.35, SD = 0.49; controls: M = 0.94,
SD = 1.70). In contrast, both language impaired groups
made a substantial number of grammatical errors (DS:
M = 7.11, SD = 2.42; SLI: M = 7.12, SD = 3.72; controls:
M = 2.44, SD = 1.58). Repeated-measures ANOVA of
mean error scores confirmed a significant group effect,
F(2, 51) = 10.281, p < .0001, 2 = .29. The repeated-measures factor was significant, F(1, 51) = 183.089, p < .0001,
2 = .78, confirming that participants made more grammatical than lexical errors. The interaction was also significant, F(2, 51) = 22.118, p < .0001, 2 = .46. Separate
ANOVAs for lexical and grammatical errors explained
this interaction: There was no group difference for lexical errors, F(2, 51) = 1.01, ns, but there was a significant group effect for grammatical errors, F(2, 51) = 18.01,
p < .0001, 2 = .41. Post hoc Scheffe tests confirmed that
both DS and SLI groups made significantly more grammatical errors than controls but did not differ from each
other.

Individual Variation in Pattern
of Language Scores
Having established comparable group language
profiles, scores were examined to establish whether individual profiles were consistent with group profiles.
Seventeen individuals with DS had poor grammar comprehension in relation to vocabulary comprehension, as
described by group data. In each case, MA-indexed standard scores were less for TROG than for BPVS-II (mean
difference = 14.7, range = 1-31). Two cases did not conform: One had the same MA-indexed standard score for
TROG as for BPVS-II; the other individual had TROG
scores three points higher than BPVS-II scores.
There was less conformity to the profile by the children with SLI. Twelve cases had poorer grammar comprehension than receptive vocabulary--the same profile as the adolescents with DS (mean difference = 9.5,
range = 3-23). Five cases had better grammar comprehension scores than receptive vocabulary scores; 3 of
them had equally impaired grammar and vocabulary; 2
had unimpaired grammar. Only 1 of these cases had pure
expressive language impairment.
Expressive language profiles showed less uniformity. Twelve cases with DS had Expressive Vocabulary scores above the criterion for impairment, but had
impaired sentence recall and/or MLU. However, 7 individuals showed no dissociation between expressive
vocabulary and grammar and met criteria for impairment on all three tests. Although 15 children with SLI
were also impaired with regard to repeating sentences
and/or MLU, only 6 children had unimpaired expressive vocabulary.

Discriminant Function Analysis
In a final statistical investigation of language profiles, discriminant function analysis (DA) was used to
establish whether the two impaired groups could be distinguished from each other, as well as from controls. The
object of DA is to classify cases into mutually exclusive
groups on the basis of an observed set of characteristics--in this case, the language measures. If language
impairments in DS and SLI are different, then DA should
produce an unambiguous classification of cases into
three separate groups: DS, SLI, and controls. If cases
cannot be readily distinguished, then "misclassification"
of cases could provide suggestive additional evidence for
equivalence between the two language impairments.
The five language test scores (BPVS-II, TROG,
CELF-R Recalling Sentences, K-ABC Expressive Vocabulary, and MLU) were entered into the DA as independent variables in stepwise fashion. On the basis of
the significant discriminant function obtained, Wilks's

 = 0.276; 2(2, N = 56) = 61.72, p < .0001, all but one of
the control cases (94.4%) were correctly classified together (see Table 2). The majority (84.2%) of the DS cases
were also classified together. However, 12 (70.6%) SLI
cases were classified with the DS cases and not in a separate group. The largest contribution to the function was
provided by the Recalling Sentences task. When the
analysis was repeated excluding this test, all cases were
classified into two groups. Control children (100%) were
grouped together, and 5 cases of DS and 6 of SLI were
also placed in this group. Eleven (64.7%) cases of SLI were
classified with the main DS group. This function was
also significant, Wilks's  = 0.655; 2(2, N = 56) = 20.27,
p < .0001, and the greatest contribution to the function
was MLU.

Small sample sizes could have limited the statistical significance of differences in morpheme use among

plausible that some of this variability could be due to
hearing differences or differences in vocabulary size.

irregular past tense verbs (e.g., ride to rode). As shown

Discussion
This study directly compared descriptions of language profiles in DS and SLI. Results showed that, like
children with SLI, young people with DS have language
skills that significantly lag behind nonverbal mental
level. Furthermore, the language profile in DS had many
similarities with that seen in SLI. First, in both receptive and expressive domains, vocabulary was an area of
relative strength, whereas syntax was poor. Second, expressive language was more severely affected than receptive language (though both were weak relative to the
control group). Third, on elicitation tasks, there were
deficits in production of grammatical markers of verb
tense. Fourth, performance was poor on tests of word
and nonword repetition, with the latter producing particularly marked deficits.
Despite the similarities, there were some intriguing points of difference between DS and SLI cases. First,
on tests of receptive and expressive vocabulary, the DS
group did not differ from MA-matched controls, whereas
the children with SLI performed worse than controls.
Second, participants with DS did not differ from controls in production of irregular past tense morphemes,

though production of regular past tense was impaired.
Children with SLI did poorly on both types of morphemes. Their errors more often involved the omission
of verb inflections, whereas individuals with DS tended
to use the wrong verb ending in elicited responses. Third,
when samples were restricted to participants who had
reached an MLU of 4.5, the group with DS did not differ
from controls in the use of either type of past tense morpheme, but children with SLI still did poorly on both
types.

Language Profiles
Given the heterogeneity evident in most large
samples of children with SLI, the extent of the similarity between the language profiles established for the
language-impaired groups in our study is worth noting.
This was evident in the analysis of group means, in the
investigation of individual language profiles, and in the
evidence for grammatical weakness provided by the
analysis of errors on the TROG test. Finding dissociation between computational and conceptual components
of language in both language-impaired groups provides
further evidence for the vulnerability to disorder in the
grammatical component of language.
Although both groups showed deficits in their understanding and use of grammar, vocabulary was a relative strength, especially for the DS group. The DS group
showed a tendency for higher receptive and expressive
vocabulary scores than did the children with SLI. This
could be attributed to their greater CA; longitudinal
studies have shown that individuals with DS continue
to learn new words into young adulthood (Chapman et
al., 1993; Fowler, 1995; Laws & Gunn, in press). The fact
that vocabulary acquisition keeps pace with nonverbal
mental abilities when there is a considerable lag in syntactic development could suggest that vocabulary is not
subject to a critical developmental period in the way that
syntax may be and that, whatever the underlying cause
of language impairment, it exerts its effect primarily on
the computational component of language.

Linguistic Deficit
Despite similar grammatical weaknesses on other
tests, a number of differences were observed in response
to the verb tense elicitation tasks. Although this study
was not designed to test different hypotheses about the
cause of morphological deficits, these observations may
be relevant to future, more theoretically motivated studies. The first difference to note was that, for a subgroup
of individuals with DS with MLU of 4.5 or more, the
number of regular and irregular past tense forms produced correctly did not significantly differ from controls.

Children with SLI and MLU > 4.5 remained impaired
on both regular and irregular past tense morpheme elicitation tasks. This might suggest that there are differences in the persistence of morphological deficits in the
two conditions.
For comparisons involving the whole samples, individuals with DS did worse than controls on regular past
tense, but they did not differ significantly from controls
in the use of irregular past tense forms. Strength in the
use of irregular past tense by participants with DS was
also noted by Eadie et al. (2002). Our study suggested
that higher levels of vocabulary in the group with DS
than in the children with SLI could account for this difference. According to Pinker (1991), irregularly inflected
forms are retrieved from associative memory just like
other vocabulary items. Thus, relatively good performance on these forms would be expected to correlate
with vocabulary level. In line with this hypothesis, there
was some indication that correct use of irregular past
tense morphemes was related to the vocabulary scores
of individuals with DS.
Eadie et al. (2002) noted an unexpected strength in
the production of regular -ed past tense forms by children with SLI, relative to MLU-matched controls. This
was not replicated here, but our comparison group was
matched for nonverbal ability and so had longer MLUs
than Eadie et al.'s controls, making it unlikely that children with SLI would approach controls' performance.
For the group with DS, our observations detected the
influence of mild to moderate hearing loss on regular ed past tense. Marginal hearing losses could have an
impact on learning regular -ed forms, where the short
duration and unstressed nature of the morpheme results in low perceptual salience. The contributions of
vocabulary knowledge and hearing to past tense use
might indicate that the morphological development of
people with DS is more closely constrained by other aspects of their development than by a specific linguistic
deficit. However, given the small sample size, the unavailability of nontense morphemes, and the fact that
this question would be better addressed by longitudinal
observation, this suggestion must be tentative.

Phonological Memory Deficit
Numerous studies have established a relationship
between nonword repetition and vocabulary acquisition
in typical development. People with DS and children
with SLI find repeating nonwords difficult, and both
groups showed substantial deficits in nonword repetition relative to controls. There was no evidence for an
association between nonword repetition and vocabulary
knowledge in the group with SLI; this was in line with
some earlier studies (Briscoe, Bishop, & Norbury, 2001;

1336

Edwards & Lahey, 1998). There was a trend toward a
relationship between nonword repetition and receptive
vocabulary in controls and in the group with DS, but
these relationships were partly accounted for by nonverbal ability. Phonological memory also contributed
significantly to the use of third-person singular tense
by the group with SLI, and other correlations between
nonword repetition and verb tense morphemes approached significance in both impaired groups (see
Table 5). This is consistent with other studies supporting a relationship between poor phonology and grammatical difficulties (Bishop et al., 2000; Conti-Ramsden,
Botting, & Faragher, 2001; Eadie et al., 2002; Ellis
Weismer et al., 2000).

Summary
Despite the differences outlined, these results are
compatible with the idea that the language impairment
found in DS is similar to the condition that affects many
children with SLI. There is also some evidence of similar underlying difficulties, particularly poor phonological memory. The similarities may be due to SLI resulting from a combination of risk factors (Bishop, 2001;
Bishop, Carlyon, Deeks, & Bishop, 1999); DS could be
just an additional, if powerful, risk factor for language
difficulties. Alternatively, the similarities could be underpinned by similar etiologies in terms of neurological
or genetic factors. Further research would be necessary
to establish whether this is so.
