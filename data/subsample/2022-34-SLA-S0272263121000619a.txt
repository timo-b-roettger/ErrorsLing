DO L1-L2 DIFFERENCES IN DISCOURSE PROCESSING REFLECT
PROCESSING DEMANDS OR DIFFICULTY OF FORM-FUNCTION
MAPPING?
EVIDENCE FROM SELF-PACED LISTENING OF CONTRASTIVE PROSODY

Eun-Kyung Lee
Yonsei University

Scott Fraundorf
University of Pittsburgh

Abstract
We examined what causes L1-L2 differences in sensitivity to prominence cues in discourse processing. Participants listened to recorded stories in segment-by-segment fashion at their own pace. Each
story established a pair of contrasting items, and one item from the pair was rementioned and
manipulated to carry either a contrastive or presentational pitch accent. By directly comparing the
current self-paced listening data to previously obtained experimenter-paced listening data, we tested
whether reducing online-processing demands allows L2 learners to show a nativelike behavior, such
that contrastive pitch accents facilitate later ruling out the salient alternative. However, reduced time
pressure failed to lead even higher proficiency L1-Korean learners of English to reach a nativelike
level, suggesting that L2 learners' nonnativelike processing and representation of the prominence cue
in spoken discourse processing can be due to the inherent difficulty of fully learning a complex formfunction mapping rather than to online-processing demands.

This question appears in many aspects of language processing, ranging from phonological

discourse processing: Given that we usually encounter sentences in context, L2 learners
must learn how to process sentences as part of a discourse beyond isolated words and
sentences to achieve a nativelike level in processing an L2. Despite some studies showing
that L2 speakers rely more on discourse-level cues than native speakers (Felser &
Cunnings, 2012; Felser et al., 2009; Pan & Felser, 2011; Pan et al., 2015; Patterson
et al., 2014), many other findings imply L2 learners have reduced sensitivity to discourselevel information compared to native speakers (e.g., Belletti et al., 2007; Cheng & Almor,

be a challenge in an L2: Using discourse-level information involves learning new formfunction mappings as well as integrating information across different domains of language. As stated by the Interface Hypothesis (Sorace, 2011), bringing multiple sources of
information together to derive an appropriate interpretation of an utterance can be
especially difficult for L2 learners.
In the present study, we investigate L2 learners' processing of one particular cue to
discourse structure: contrastive pitch accents, which past work has shown that even
relatively proficient L2 learners do not process in a fully nativelike way (e.g., Braun &
Tagliapietra, 2011; Lee & Fraundorf, 2017; Lee et al., 2020; Perdomo & Kaan, 2021). We
review differences in how these cues are used in L1 and L2 discourse processing before
we consider competing explanations of L1-L2 differences: L2 learners do not have the
online-processing capability to consider pitch accenting cues, or L2 learners have not
fully acquired the difficult mapping between pitch accent and information status. Then,
we introduce our self-paced listening task, which can test whether L2 learners show
nonnativelike processing of contrastive pitch accents even when online-processing
demands are reduced.
PROMINENCE CUES IN L1 DISCOURSE PROCESSING

Discourse comprehension requires understanding and representing the information structure of an utterance--for example, which information is new or focused in the discourse,
and which information is given or backgrounded. The information structure of the
discourse can be signaled by various linguistic devices, such as word order variation,
morphological marking, and prosody (see Arnold et al., 2013, for review). For instance, in
English spoken discourse, one cue that can indicate discourse structure is pitch accenting,
a phonological construct realized acoustically with lengthening and increased pitch and
intensity. At least two types of pitch accents make different contributions to the information structure of an utterance (Ladd, 2008). The H* presentational pitch accent, which
involves a high tonal target, is thought to be associated primarily with new information
whereas the LH* contrastive pitch accent, which involves a high tonal target preceded
by a steep rise from a low tone, is thought to signal a contrast between an entity and its
alternatives (Pierrehumbert & Hirschberg, 1990; Watson et al., 2008). For example, in the
discourse "John gave me a BOOK. The book had a pretty cover. Then he gave me a
PENCIL," the presence of an H* presentational pitch accent on the first occurrence of
book and pencil mark them as new information. By contrast, in the sentence "Open the

944 Eun-Kyung and Scott Fraundorf
RED door, not the green door," the speaker intends to contrast the red door with its
alternative, the green door, by producing an LH* contrastive pitch accent on the color
adjective. Using a visual world eye-tracking study, Watson et al. (2008) demonstrated
that, while the H* pitch accent may sometimes also be interpreted contrastively, the
LH* pitch accent leads specifically to a contrastive reading.
Ample evidence now suggests that, for native speakers, contrastive pitch accents
contribute to successful discourse comprehension. Contrastive pitch accents facilitate
online reference resolution, in the face of temporarily ambiguous linguistic input, by
favoring referents with a contrastive relation (e.g., a RED ball when there is also a blue
ball; Arnold, 2008; Dahan et al., 2002; Ito & Speer, 2008; Ito et al., 2012; Nakamura et al.,
2012; Watson et al., 2008; Weber et al., 2006). They also influence native speakers'
offline memory for a discourse by enhancing the representation of a specific contrast item
(Fraundorf et al., 2013; Fraundorf et al., 2010, 2012).

PROMINENCE CUES IN L2 DISCOURSE PROCESSING

But it is also known that L2 learners have difficulty achieving a nativelike level in making
use of contrastive pitch accents to enhance online language processing (e.g., Akker &
Cutler, 2003; Braun & Tagliapietra, 2011; Chen & Lai, 2011; Lee et al., 2020; Nakamura
et al., 2020; Perdomo & Kaan, 2021). For example, Nakamura et al. (2020) found that
Japanese-speaking L2 learners of English failed to interpret LH* contrastive pitch
accents in online reference resolution as a cue to contrast as native English speakers
do. A visual world eye-tracking study by Perdomo and Kaan (2021) showed that L2
learners could establish a relevant contrast set in response to contrastive pitch accents
when options for the contrast set were restricted by the visual display. However, L2
learners failed to use contrastive pitch accents predictively, as native speakers did. The
authors manipulated the presence of a contrastive pitch accent on possessive proper nouns
for sentences including two characters and two objects (e.g., We ate Angela's ice cream
but saved Benjamin's/BENJAMIN'S cake in the fridge). For native English speakers, a
contrastive pitch accent biased eye fixations toward the same object as the previously
mentioned one (e.g., ice cream) even before the critical noun (e.g., cake) was heard. Like
native speakers, L2 learners were more likely to fixate at the same object (e.g., ice cream)
when the proper noun was accented than when it was not. However, for L2 learners, this
difference did not come about until after the critical noun (e.g., cake) was heard,
suggesting that L2 learners' use of contrastive pitch accents was not as efficient as that
of native speakers.
L2 learners' nonnativelike processing of contrastive pitch accents is also reflected in
their later memory for prominent information (Lee & Fraundorf, 2017). Consider the
discourse in (1) in the following text. Emphasizing British in (1) with a contrastive pitch
accent may lead native English speakers to retain as part of their discourse representation
not just the true proposition that the British found one of the monkeys but also the salient
contrastive alternative French. This is seen in the fact that, relative to an H* presentational
pitch accent, the LH* contrastive pitch accent led to a higher rate of correct rejections
only of a memory probe referring to the specific contrastive alternative French (The
French scientists found the endangered monkey) but not a probe about an unmentioned

However, in representing a spoken discourse, L2 learners do not derive the same benefits
from contrastive pitch accents as do L1 speakers. Lee and Fraundorf (2017) tested
Korean-speaking L2 English learners' memory for an L2 discourse like (1) in the
preceding text. They found that low- and mid-proficiency L2 learners did not show any
memory effects at all of contrastive pitch accenting. And, for high-proficiency learners,
the contrastive pitch accent did affect memory, but not by facilitating rejections of a
memory probe about the contrast item. It instead facilitated rejections of a memory probe
about an unmentioned item over and above the H* pitch accent. The finding indicates that
high proficiency L2 learners were indeed sensitive to different pitch accent types and that
the LH* contrastive pitch accent helped them distinguish an unmentioned item from the
set of alternatives. However, the fact that contrastive pitch accents did not lead high
proficiency L2 learners to correctly rule out the salient alternative suggests that the
representation constructed by L2 learners in response to contrastive pitch accents did
not distinguish which is the contrastive alternative from which is the correct item as fully
as that of native speakers.
In summary, there is evidence that, empirically, L2 learners do not capitalize on a cue to
contrast in spoken discourse in the same way as L1 speakers do. Here, we consider the
mechanisms that might explain such a difference.

WHAT DRIVES L2 PROCESSING DIFFERENCES?

A long-standing contrast in the L2 literature is between representational and processing
accounts of L1-L2 differences. That is, although L1-L2 difference could arise if L2
learners had not acquired the same knowledge of the language as L1 speakers (or had not
acquired it yet), it is also possible that L1-L2 differences could be observed even if L2
learners had similar knowledge of the language to that of L1 speakers but were not able to
apply it in the experimental tasks--for instance, because they are slower in their online
processing. The Interface Hypothesis (Sorace, 2011) posits limited processing resources
as the source of L2 learners' difficulties integrating multiple kinds of information for
interpretation. In principle, this could explain L2 learners' nonnativelike interpretation of
contrastive pitch accents described in the preceding text because they require an interface
between different levels of linguistic representation, that is, prosody and discourse
structure.
Evidence that L2 learners' limited processing resources may indeed underlie L1-L2
processing differences comes from the finding that even native speakers' behavior may
become similar to that of nonnative speakers in cognitively demanding situations (Hopp,
2009; Kilborn, 1992; MacDonald, 2006). For example, Kilborn (1992) compared L2
speakers' behavior with that of native speakers on a word monitoring task and found that
advanced L2 speakers failed to integrate syntactic information with semantics as rapidly
as native speakers during online comprehension. Crucially, native speakers showed a

946 Eun-Kyung and Scott Fraundorf
similar performance to that of L2 speakers when they performed the task in the presence of
background noise. Hopp (2009) demonstrated that native speakers' behavior became
similar to that of nonnative speakers in processing inflection when time pressure imposed
a cognitive load. These data suggest that, in several other aspects of L2 language
processing (e.g., syntactic and semantic processing), nonnativelike performance may
be caused by increased cognitive load and reduced processing efficiency and that L1-L2
differences are quantitative rather than qualitative.
Does the processing demand account also apply to L2 comprehension at the levels of
discourse and prosodic processing? Some offline studies have shown that, when onlineprocessing demands were minimized, L2 learners were similar to native speakers in
interpreting L2 contrastive pitch accents as a signal to contrast (e.g., Takahashi et al.,
2018; Takeda, 2018). For example, Takahashi et al. (2018) found that Mandarin-speaking
L2 learners of English were faster to identify a referent that was contrastive in color with
the previously activated referent (e.g., scarlet mittens) when a contrastive pitch accent was
produced appropriately (e.g., Click on the purple mittens. Now click on the SCARLET
mittens), compared to when it was used infelicitously (e.g., Click on the purple necklace.
Now click on the SCARLET mittens). This finding suggests that, just like native speakers,
L2 learners can infer the discourse status of referents from L2 contrastive pitch accents if
they are provided with sufficient time to establish a relevant contrast set. The fact that L2
speakers behave like native speakers in untimed offline tasks when they do not in real-time
online tasks suggests that nonnativelike processing of contrastive pitch accents could
arise from online-processing demands rather than from a failure to learn the discourse
meaning of contrastive pitch accents.
However, the evidence reviewed in the preceding text stems from offline judgment
tasks. An even stronger test of the processing demand account is that, even in some onlineprocessing tasks, L1-L2 differences in discourse processing should be negligible so long
as there is sufficient time for L2 learners to integrate all the relevant cues. Support for this
prediction comes from Lee and Fraundorf (2019), who found that L2 learners can make
nativelike use of other cues to prominence--namely, font emphasis, such as italicization
or capitalization in written text, which for native speakers has been observed to promote
representation of salient alternatives similarly to contrastive pitch accents (Fraundorf
et al., 2013). The authors had L1-Korean learners of L2-English read--rather than listen
to--discourses like (1) in which critical words were manipulated for font emphasis
(i.e., capitalized or not). Critically, reading was self-paced, so L2 learners had as much
time as desired to process the texts. In this task, font emphasis enhanced memory
specifically by facilitating rejections of a probe about the contrastive alternative, just as
for native speakers. One interpretation of these results is that nonnativelike encoding of
contrastive pitch accents may not be due to intrinsic L1-L2 differences in encoding salient
alternatives in response to prominence cues in general, but rather the cognitive demands
of processing a prominence cue online in addition to words and syntax of running speech
during listening (Lee & Fraundorf, 2017); thus, when these online-processing demands
were minimized using self-paced reading, L2 learners could make appropriate use of
prominence cues.
Nevertheless, an alternate interpretation of the preceding results is that L2 learners
show nativelike comprehension of font emphasis (Lee & Fraundorf, 2019) but not
prosodic cues (Lee & Fraundorf, 2017) simply because the meaning of font emphasis

is easier to learn. In general, the complex-mapping hypothesis (Patterson et al., 2017;
Schafer et al., 2015) states that nativelike discourse processing depends on the complexity
of the form-function mapping. For instance, Patterson et al. (2017) examined L2 learners'
processing of cues to pronoun resolution. In German and Russian, L1 comprehenders
show an "antifocus" effect such that within-sentence ambiguous pronouns are preferentially understood as referring to topicalized entities rather than a clefted (i.e., focused)
subject because topichood is considered as a signal of givenness. Despite the fact that
German and Russian have a similar focus structure, German-speaking L2 learners of
Russian and Russian-speaking L2 learners of German did not show this antifocus effect,
which Patterson et al. (2017) interpreted as reflecting the complex mapping of syntactic
structure to information structure and information structure to pronoun resolution. Schafer
et al. (2015) also showed that nativelike discourse processing depends on the complexity
of the mapping. The authors found that L2 learners' pronoun resolution was nativelike
when potential antecedents were produced with a contrastive pitch accent (story continuation task, David/DAVID served/was serving Paul/PAUL a pint of beer. He...): pronouns were associated with an accented antecedent irrespective of whether it was assigned
the thematic role of source or goal. However, L2 learners failed to show a nativelike
performance in interpreting accented pronouns (David served/was serving Paul a pint of
beer. He/HE obviously...), which required a more complex computation in which the
accessibility of two potential referents is compared and the pronoun is associated with the
less prominent one.
This complex-mapping hypothesis could also explain prior results attributed to processing demands if the typeface convention of font emphasis presents a form-function
mapping that is easier to learn than that of contrastive pitch accents. The mapping between
specific pitch accent types and specific discourse functions is language-specific and is not
explicitly taught in second language classrooms. Recent work also demonstrates that such
a mapping is not one-to-one but has inter- and intraspeaker variation (Grice et al., 2017).
Thus, L2 learners may experience difficulty learning L2 mappings between specific
prosodic forms and functions, especially when those mappings do not match those of
L1 (Braun & Tagliapietra, 2011; Chen & Lai, 2011; Namjoshi, 2015; Ortega-Llebaria &
Colantoni, 2014). Further, one of the acoustic properties that distinguish among different
types of pitch accenting is the pattern of pitch excursion. However, pitch excursion is used
to signal not only information structure but also syntactic structure or speech acts
(Pierrehumbert & Hirschberg, 1990). For these reasons, the form-function mapping of
contrastive pitch accents may require a more complex computation, which could make it
difficult for L2 learners to learn. This may be especially true for Korean L2 learners,
whose L1 does not use contrastive pitch accents as a signal to contrast (Jun, 1993, 2005).
Thus, it is possible that the inherent difficulty of grasping the exact function of the
contrastive pitch accents results in the L1-L2 difference in the way the contrastive
information is encoded and represented in memory. This view does not necessarily
assume that L2 learners would be completely insensitive to the information conveyed
by contrastive pitch accents. For instance, L2 learners may construct an alternative set
online in response to contrastive pitch accents but still fail to use that information
efficiently to make local predictions (Perdomo & Kaan, 2021) or to represent it in memory
in a nativelike way, distinguishing what is the salient alternative from what is the critical
referent (Lee & Fraundorf, 2017). On this view, reducing online-processing demands

948 Eun-Kyung and Scott Fraundorf
might not be sufficient for leading to a nativelike behavior unless L2 learners had a
sufficient amount of naturalistic exposure to fully acquire to process and encode contrastive pitch accents.
PRESENT STUDY

The present study explores what gives rise to L1-L2 differences in sensitivity to cues in
discourse processing, here examining the memory effects of contrastive pitch accents. We
tested whether L2 learners are able to show a nativelike behavior in processing and
representing in memory the information conveyed by contrastive pitch accents if onlineprocessing demands are minimized; that is, if the task allows them to take as long as they
fully derive a contrastive interpretation and encode salient alternatives while listening. To
do so, we adopted a self-paced listening task, which allows participants as much time as
desired to process the discourses, just as in reading. We compare the results obtained
under this task to previously obtained data on experimenter-paced listening with the same
prominence cue and the same materials (Fraundorf et al., 2010; Lee & Fraundorf, 2017),
which had revealed differences between L1 speakers and proficient L2 learners; this
comparison between self- and experimenter-paced listening effectively varies time
pressure across studies.
If L2 learners' nonnativelike processing and representation of contrastive pitch accents
were indeed due to online-processing demands (i.e., the processing-demand hypothesis),
we would expect their behavior to converge with that of native speakers in situations in
which online-processing demands are reduced. By contrast, if the conflicting results
across studies arose from the inherent difficulty of learning a complex form-function
mapping of contrastive pitch accents (i.e., the complex-mapping hypothesis), L2 learners'
performance on the self-paced listening task should not differ from their performance on
the experimenter-paced listening task.

PARTICIPANTS

Participants were 62 native English speakers (mean age: 19.02, 22 males, 40 females) and
68 native Korean speakers who learned English as an L2 (mean age: 23.8, 35 males,
30 females). Three L2 participants were excluded due to technical error. Native English
speakers were undergraduate students at the University of Pittsburgh and participated in
the study for course credit. The L2 participants were undergraduate or graduate students at
Yonsei University and were paid for their participation. Their length of stay in an Englishspeaking country before the age of 15 was no longer than 6 months. We assessed L2
participants' English proficiency using a multiple-choice cloze test (Lee & Fraundorf,

MATERIALS

The stimulus materials consisted of 36 recorded stories taken from Experiment 3 of
Fraundorf et al. (2010). Each story began with a context passage, such as (2a) in the
following text, that established two pairs of contrasting alternatives (here, Grand Canyon
vs. Everglades and spring vs. fall). Then, each story continued with a continuation
passage, such as (2b), that referred to one member from each pair, which we term the
critical words.
(2a) Steve and his wife had been wanting to visit the Grand Canyon and the Everglades. Steve's
employer told him he could take a week of vacation either in the spring or in the fall.
(2b) Steve and his family considered their options before eventually deciding to visit the Grand
Canyon in the spring.

The stories were recorded by a native female speaker of American English with an Inland
North American accent (Labov et al., 2006). We manipulated whether each of the two
critical words in the continuation passage was heard with a presentational (H*) or a
contrastive (LH*) pitch accent, such that each continuation passage could be heard with
a contrastive pitch accent on the first critical word, on the second, on both, or neither. (The
materials were originally constructed to have two contrast sets per passage to test whether
a contrastive pitch accent on one critical word would affect memory for the other.
However, Fraundorf et al. (2010) report no such effects, so we collapse over this variable
in the present analyses.) Pitch accenting was manipulated using auditory cross-splicing so
that all audio except the critical words was held constant across conditions. Table 2
(Fraundorf et al., 2010, p. 376) presents means and standard errors of acoustic measure
verifying that the LH* and H* conditions differed.
To test participants' memory for the stories, two sets of probe statements were
constructed for each story--one for each critical word in the continuation, such as
(3a)-(3c) in the following text for Grand Canyon and (4a)-(4c) for spring. Each critical
word could be tested with either a correct probe describing what happened in the story (3a,
4a), a false contrastive probe in which the correct critical word was replaced by its salient
alternative (3b, 4b), or a false unmentioned probe in which the critical word was replaced
with a word that was same from the semantic category but that was never mentioned in any
of the original stories (3c, 4c). Participants were tested separately on each of the two
critical words from every text; the probe statements were constructed so that the probe for

PROCEDURE

The experiment comprised a study phase and a test phase. Unlike Fraundorf et al. (2010),
where native speakers were tested with a 24-hour lag between study and test, the test phase

immediately followed the study phase as in Lee and Fraundorf (2017). Participants were
instructed in English that they would listen to 36 short stories and that their memory for
these stories would be tested after listening to all of them.
For the study phase, we used a self-paced listening task. Participants were told to listen
to the stories in a segment-by-segment fashion at their own pace. Each of the 36 stories
were segmented into prosodic phrases so that it would have natural segment boundaries as
illustrated in (4) in the following text (double slashes indicate segment boundaries). The
average number of segments per story was 10.3 across stories (range: 6-17). The two
critical words of each story (e.g., Grand Canyon and spring) always appeared in separate
segments. The stories were presented in a randomized order.
(4) Steve and his wife had been wanting to visit the Grand Canyon // and the Everglades. // Steve's
employer told him // he could take a week of vacation // either in the spring // or in the fall. //
Steve and his family considered their options // before eventually deciding to visit the Grand
Canyon // in the spring.

Although the stories were presented only in auditory form, we did display a visual
indicator of participants' progress (see Figure 1). Each trial began with the display of a
set of lines, arranged in paragraph form, matched in length to each of the words in the

representing the corresponding words switched to crosses. Participants had to press the
space bar to listen to the next segment. Once the next segment began, the crosses in the
previous segment were reverted to lines. This display accomplished two purposes: (a) it
indicated to participants their progress through each story, which could be rather lengthy,
and (b) it closely matched the display of the typical moving-window reading paradigm
(except without actual words being displayed), allowing us to compare our present results

keyboard. Test stimuli were composed of 72 statements about the stories that they had
heard in the study phase (one statement per critical word). We instructed participants to
judge the statement as being false if any part of it was not exactly true. The order of the test
statements was rerandomized into a different order from the study phase.
RESULTS

We measured online listening time from the offset of each segment to the onset of key
press so that it indexed time spent to process the segment before moving on to the
subsequent segment. A preliminary analysis indicated no significant effect of accent type
on online listening time for both L1 and L2 speakers, indicating that neither group
required extra time to process prominent words beyond the time provided by contrastive
pitch accents.
Our primary measure of interest was in participants' responses to the memory test. One
challenge in interpreting these responses is that the correct answer varied across conditions: "true" for the correct probes, but "false" for the contrast and unmentioned probes.
Thus, differences across conditions in the rate of correct responses may be driven in part
by an overall bias to respond "true" or "false."
Signal-detection analyses (Macmillan & Creelman, 2005) have recently been suggested as a solution to this problem in language processing (Huang & Ferreira, 2020).
These analyses have long been used in the study of visual psychophysics (Green & Swets,
1966) and--relevant to our present investigation--long-term memory (Banks, 1970).
(See Murayama et al., 2014, and Wright et al., 2008, for applications to multilevel models
using the log odds ratio; see also Fraundorf et al., 2013; Fraundorf et al., 2010; James et al.,
2018; Lee & Fraundorf, 2017, 2019; Tokowicz & MacWhinney, 2005 for applications to
language processing.) In a detection-theoretic analysis, the dependent variable is not the
rate of accuracy but the type of response given; that is, did a particular participant judge a
particular probe as being "true" (or "false")? The response given can then be examined as
a function of conceptually and empirically dissociable influences: (a) a participant's
response bias to respond "true" or "false" (the overall rate of "true" responses) and
(b) their sensitivity to whether individual probes are true or false (responding "true" more
when the probe is in fact true).
To examine whether reduced time pressure helped L2 learners process and represent
contrastive pitch accents in a nativelike manner, we directly compared the native speakers
and L2 learners tested on the self-paced listening task here to participants previously
tested on the experimenter-paced listening task (Fraundorf et al., 2010; Lee & Fraundorf,
2017).3 To this end, we first fit a mixed logit model (Jaeger, 2008) with an intercept term,
probe type (correct vs. contrast vs. unmentioned), accent type (H* vs. LH*), task
(experimenter-paced listening vs. self-paced listening), group (L1 vs. L2), and their
interactions as fixed effects. In this model, the intercept term captures response bias; that
is, the overall rate of responding "true." Sensitivity to whether a probe statement is
veridically true is captured by including the probe type as an independent variable. That is,

Encoding L2 Contrastive Accents

953

by combining all three probe types into a single model, we can test whether people
respond "true" more for the probe types that indeed represent true statements. Specifically, the probe type variable was effect-coded for two planned comparisons. In one
comparison, the rate of "true" responses to contrast probes was compared to the grand
mean of "true" responses. In the other comparison, the rate of "true" responses to
unmentioned probes was compared to the grand mean of "true" responses. The other
variables (accent type, task, and group) serve as influences on sensitivity when they
interact with probe and as influences on response bias when they do not; these variables
were coded using mean-centered contrast coding.
This and the following models all had by-participant and by-item random intercepts as
random effects. Random slopes were added to the random effects structure only when the
model fit improved (p's> .05).

DISCUSSION

Using a self-paced listening task, we explored what gives rise to L1-L2 differences in
encoding and representing a salient alternative in response to contrastive pitch accents. In
particular, we tested whether allowing participants sufficient time to process spoken
discourse, using self-paced listening, might lead to more nativelike patterns of discourse
comprehension. We directly compared memory performance on the self-paced listening
task to earlier studies using an experimenter-paced listening task (Fraundorf et al., 2010;

960 Eun-Kyung and Scott Fraundorf
Lee & Fraundorf, 2017), which did not necessarily give participants as much time as
desired to process the discourse.
For native English speakers, contrastive pitch accents benefited memory by facilitating rejections of statements about the contrastive alternative, just as in the
experimenter-paced listening task. Note that the L1, experimenter-paced listening
task had a different retention interval than the other tasks. This difference is indeed a
confound and may account for the fact that native speakers showed better overall
memory performance in the self-paced listening task than in the experimenter-paced
listening task. However, the question of interest here was not whether and how task
type affects overall memory performance. The current study focuses instead on
whether task type modulates how contrastive pitch accents, in particular, affect later
memory for discourse. Previous L1 studies showed that the difference in retention
interval had no impact on the contrastive-accent effect in L1 processing. While
Experiment 3 of Fraundorf et al. (2010), in which our L1 data for the
experimenter-paced listening task originated, tested native speakers' recognition
memory with a 24-hour lag between study and test, other L1 studies looking at
mnemonic effects of prominence cues on memory for discourse (Fraundorf et al.,
2010, 2012, 2013) had the test phase immediately after the study phase just as in our
self-paced listening task. Despite this difference in the length of retention interval, the
effects of contrastive pitch accents on salient alternatives were replicated across
studies. Crucially, the current data revealed that native speakers derived the same
benefits from contrastive pitch accents across tasks. The fact that we replicated the
previous finding using a self-paced listening task suggests that the task used here was
sensitive enough to capture the comprehenders' sensitivity to contrastive pitch
accents.
But, even in the self-paced listening task, L2 listeners did not show a nativelike
behavior. For higher proficiency L2 learners, we found a mnemonic benefit of
contrastive pitch accents: The content of the discourse was better recalled when the
critical word had been produced with a contrastive pitch accent than when it had not.
However, higher proficiency L2 leaners differed from native speakers in how contrastive pitch accents affected later memory for discourse. Just as in the experimenterpaced listening task, contrastive pitch accents helped rejections of statements only
about the unmentioned item, and not about the contrast item. This result indicates that
higher proficiency L2 learners brought to mind the contrast between the critical word
and its salient alternative in response to contrastive pitch accents, but they failed to
distinguish the contrastive alternative from the correct item in their memory representation of the discourse. For lower proficiency L2 learners, reduced time pressure had an
effect of improving overall memory for discourse, but this effect was not modulated by
pitch accent type.
In sum, the data show that--contrary to the processing-demand hypothesis--the
contrastive pitch accents failed to lead even higher proficiency L2 learners to process
and represent the discourse in a nativelike way even in situations in which they were
allowed to process the discourse under no time constraint. The fact that having more time
for the task did not lead to a nativelike behavior suggests that L2 learners' nonnativelike
encoding and representation of contrastive pitch accents are not attributable solely to
online-processing demands.

UNDERSTANDING PROMINENCE IN L2 ENGLISH

As discussed in the preceding text, contrastive pitch accents helped higher proficiency L2
learners later rule out the unmentioned entity. This suggests that higher proficiency L2
learners were not insensitive to contrastive pitch accents. They were able to derive a
contrastive interpretation to some extent. Nevertheless, a nonnativelike behavior resulted
from the failure to fully integrate the salient alternative into their discourse representation.
One might argue that L2 learners could have difficulty representing the contrastive
alternative in a nativelike way because they did not have strong lexical representations for
the words in contrast sets. However, there are reasons to think that the strength of the
representation of the referents alone is not sufficient to account for the absence of a
nativelike effect of contrastive pitch accents. First, we looked up the word frequency of
the two words in each of 65 out of 72 pairs of alternatives.4 The mean log frequencies of
the two members (SUBTLEXUS corpus; Brysbaert and New, 2009) of the contrast sets
were similar (first contrast set: 2.8 vs. 2.8; second contrast set: 2.9 vs. 3.0). This suggests
that, at least, there is no a priori reason for participants to be more familiar with the correct
target than the salient alternative. Second, Lee and Fraundorf (2019) used similar critical
words to those used in the present study, and found that L2 learners represented a written
discourse in response to font emphasis in the same way as native speakers did. Third, the
current results demonstrated that higher proficiency L2 participants had some degree of
veridical memory for the discourse: both types of incorrect probes were less likely to be
judged as being true compared to their overall rate of "true" response. This suggests that
higher proficiency L2 participants did not have much difficulty understanding the literal
text. Finally, the contrastive pitch accent helped higher proficiency L2 learners reject only
the statements about an unmentioned item. This indicates that they did distinguish the
contrastive alternatives from other items that were not part of the contrast set, suggesting
that higher proficiency L2 learners had indeed brought the contrast set to the mind. Rather,
this result suggests that they failed to reject the contrastive alternative because of the
failure to encode which of the two items in the contrast set was the contrastive alternative.

THE COMPLEX-MAPPING HYPOTHESIS

The current result stands in contrast to previous findings (Lee & Fraundorf, 2019) that L2
learners showed nativelike use of font emphasis to encode and represent a discourse in
memory. This suggests that the nonnativelike behavior tied specifically to contrastive
pitch accents is not due simply to L2 learners' difficulty of mapping prominence cues onto
the structure of L2 discourse. Integrating multiple sources of information is not always
challenging for L2 learners. What is particularly challenging for L2 learners seems to be
learning the exact function of a specific type of prominence cue, that is, the contrastive
pitch accent, or at least using it to make inferences about salient alternatives.
The discrepancy in results between previous work (Lee & Fraundorf, 2019) and the
current study lends support to the complex-mapping hypothesis by implying that L2
learners' nativelike discourse processing depends on the complexity of the form-function
mapping of prominence cues. In Lee and Fraundorf (2019), L2 learners showed nativelike
processing of font emphasis, which could be acquired relatively easily as a typeface
convention. By comparison, in the present study, the form-function mapping of

962 Eun-Kyung and Scott Fraundorf
contrastive pitch accents is more complicated because it is language-specific, and pitch
movements may signal functions other than marking information structure. Thus, the
finding that contrastive pitch accents failed to lead L2 learners to distinguish what was the
correct item from what was the salient alternative--even when online-processing
demands were reduced--suggests that contrastive pitch accents are inherently difficult
to learn to process as a cue for L2 learners, presumably because of their complex formfunction mapping.
What about the past offline tasks that did show nativelike processing of contrastive
pitch accents? We note that these tasks not only lacked time pressure, but they were also
accompanied by visual contexts. The presence of visual contexts could lead L2 learners to
process contrastive pitch accents in a nativelike way by helping them make inferences
about the information structure as well as reducing memory load (e.g., Takahashi et al.,
2018; Takeda, 2018). In other words, visual contexts likely helped L2 participants
compute the pragmatic function of contrastive pitch accents. By contrast, previous online
tasks that failed to show a nativelike performance either provided no visual context or
used a visual context that required a complex computation (e.g., Akker & Cutler, 2003;
Lee et al., 2020; Nakamura et al., 2020). For example, Nakamura et al. (2020) found that
Japanese-speaking L2 learners of English failed to interpret LH* contrastive pitch
accents in online reference resolution as a cue to contrast as native English speakers
do. The task required participants to interpret LH* contrastive pitch accents in a contextdependent way: as a cue to contrast only in a two-referent context and not in a one-referent
context. L2 learners' nonnativelike behavior in this particular study may be attributed at
least in part to the demands of integrating visual and prosodic information, which made it
difficult for them to derive an appropriate interpretation from contrastive pitch accents,
rather than to a deficit in prosodic processing per se. Thus, the difference in ease of
computing a form-function mapping of contrastive pitch accents could underlie the
seemingly conflicting findings between prior online and offline studies.
A FUNDAMENTAL REPRESENTATIONAL DEFICIT?

The present study demonstrates that nonnativelike encoding and representation of contrastive pitch accents can lead to L1-L2 differences in spoken discourse comprehension.
The data show that L2 learners fail to reach a nativelike level in integrating the salient
alternative into their discourse representation in response to contrastive pitch accents even
in less temporally demanding situations. However, it is still not clear whether the L1-L2
difference reported here reflects a fundamental representational deficit. Evidence against a
fundamental difference comes from studies showing that L2 learners' performance varies
by task demands or L2 proficiency level or that native speakers' performance becomes
nonnativelike as a function of increased cognitive demands (Frenck-Mestre, 2002; Hopp,
2006, 2009, 2010; Jackson, 2008; Jackson & Bobb, 2009; Jackson & Dussias, 2009;
Kilborn, 1992; McDonald, 2006). Another piece of evidence comes from studies focusing
on L2 learners with extensive naturalistic L2 exposure (Pliatsikas & Marinis, 2013). The
shallow structure hypothesis argues that filler-gap processing requires a complex computation of syntax and, thus, it is one of the domains for which a fundamental L1-L2
difference might exist (Clahsen & Felser, 2006). Nevertheless, Pliatsikas and Marinis
demonstrated that L2 learners with extensive naturalistic L2 exposure showed a filler-gap

Encoding L2 Contrastive Accents

963

effect similar to that observed for native English speakers while those with limited
naturalistic exposure did not, indicating that the input provided through naturalistic
exposure made it possible for L2 learners to construct a detailed syntactic representation
that included empty categories just as native speakers did.
In the current study, we only tested L2 learners who had no or limited naturalistic L2
exposure. Forty-four out of 65 L2 learners who participated in the study had never lived in
an L2 environment. For the remaining 21 participants, only four had an experience of
staying in an English-speaking country for longer than 6 months. Given that the formfunction mapping of contrastive pitch accents is not explicitly taught in L2-English
classroom and that it may be only acquired through extensive exposure to L2 input, only
sufficient amount of naturalistic exposure may provide the input sufficient for fully
learning native-like encoding and representation of contrastive pitch accents.
Thus, although we have evidence that there is an L1-L2 difference in response to
contrastive pitch accents, we cannot conclude whether this representational deficit is a
fundamental one or a quantitative one that could be alleviated with further experience.
That question could only be answered in future research that tests L2 learners with
extensive naturalistic exposure--more than any of our present participants--and determines whether their performance converges with that of native speakers.

CONCLUSION

Our results demonstrate that higher proficiency Korean-speaking L2 learners of English
show some sensitivity to contrastive pitch accents during spoken discourse comprehension, but still fail to fully integrate into memory which was the salient alternative even
when online-processing demands are reduced. This finding, combined with previous
evidence for nativelike comprehension of font emphasis (Lee & Fraundorf, 2019),
suggests that what might underlie nonnativelike processing of contrastive pitch accents
is L2 learners' failure to fully acquire the difficult mapping between pitch accent and
information status rather than the lack of the online-processing capability to encode and
represent the salient alternative in response to prominence cues. More generally, this work
supports the complex-mapping hypothesis (Patterson et al., 2017; Schafer et al., 2015)
that L2 leaners' ability to acquire and use cues to L2 discourse processing depends in part
on the complexity of the required form-meaning mapping.
