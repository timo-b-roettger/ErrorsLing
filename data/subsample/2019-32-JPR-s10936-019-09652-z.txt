Abstract
A long history exists of the utilization of narratives to help young people cope with stress
and illness. Research is beginning to focus on video based story-telling for the purpose of
intervention, however little research has been conducted to look at factors that influence
the narrative content of these films. As a pilot, and within the context of a larger study,
the current research was conducted with n = 10 adolescents with cancer and n = 10 healthy
peers exploring participant characteristics (e.g. personality, quality of life, etc.) and their
relationship with linguistic consent of the film. Despite little to no differences identified
in demographic characteristics, results identified distinct differences between each group,
suggesting that linguistic aspects of film narratives differ in adolescents with and without
cancer. This research can serve to motivate future directions of exploration surrounding the
content of film narratives and their relationship to patient well-being.
Keywords Pediatric cancer * Video narrative * Illness narrative * Adolescents * AYA*
Therapeutic filmmaking

Introduction
It goes without saying that at this stage of our field's history, it is well-known that talking about one's negative experiences can bring healing and relief from emotional distress.
With respect to trauma, reciting or writing one's trauma narrative is thought to be a particularly important aspect of confronting and processing difficult experiences. As the recognition of this important intervention has grown, the process of telling one's narrative has
been explored to identify what makes this practice beneficial (Frattaroli 2006). Additionally, the use of linguistics has had growing popularity in recent years; especially with the
advent of the internet and thus easy access to a host of written material (Liess et al. 2008).
Researchers have found that changes in narrative language are directly related to one's
overall emotional health as well as their perspective of the event being discussed (Tausczik

Cancer Narratives
With respect to the narrative content, the thematic and linguistic ways people talk about
feelings and events are indicative and predictive of how they are coping and experiencing
the world (Tausczik and Pennebaker 2010; Pyszczynski and Greenberg 1987). Research
has demonstrated that cancer patients, particularly those with childhood cancers, are likely
to display symptoms of emotional trauma and stress (Cordova et al. 1995). Self-disclosure,
including through written narratives, has been shown to mitigate psychological distress following exposure to traumatic events (Pennebaker and Harber 1993). For patients with cancer, having a safe medium to discuss their emotions surrounding their illness or treatment
has been useful in helping alleviate symptoms related to emotional well-being and trauma
(Barnett et al. 2014; Henry et al. 2010). Openness and disclosure about cancer status has
been shown to be related to higher levels of well-being (Park et al. 2005). Other studies
have demonstrated the benefits of written narratives for cancer patients including improvement in physical symptoms (Stanton et al. 2002), better sleep quality (Didden et al. 2002),
lower levels of pain (Rosenberg et al. 2002), and greater perceived social support (Gellaitry
et al. 2009). More generally, a filmmaking program for creative self-disclosure in trauma
survivors has found an increase in agency, affiliation and processing of their trauma (TuvalMashiach et al. 2018).

Adolescent and Young Adult Populations and Narratives
Adolescents and young adults are a unique age group within the cancer population because
of their emotional and social development during this period in their lives. While adults
with cancer may experience negative emotions surrounding treatment-related physical
changes, adolescents are still developing emotionally and cognitively, leaving them at
higher risk for negative outcomes, including how they develop a sense of self (Aamodt
et al. 1984). Establishment of personal identity is essential to all young people, especially
those with cancer and McAdams (2001) proposes that one's identity is a life story which
begins to form during adolescence. Young adults with cancer also have age-related developmental tasks, such as developing autonomy, making independent choices about employment, pursing education, maintaining relationships and starting a family, that are interrupted because of disease and treatment (Zebrack et al. 2012). This critical transitional
period, from childhood into adulthood, also leaves adolescents and young adults at risk to
experience more distress from age-specific emotional and physical challenges when diagnosed with cancer (National Cancer Institute 2012). Young people with cancer must endure
a number of challenges including invasive, repetitive procedures and treatments, side
effects of these life saving measures, changes in family routines and roles, changes in relationships and responsibilities, as well as a multitude of feelings surrounding their illness
(Woodgate 2001). Travagin et al. (2015) conducted a meta-analysis to evaluate the effects
of self-disclosure through narratives of healthy adolescents. Results found that adolescents

The use of narratives within the Adolescent and Young Adult (AYA) population has
been a growing interest for researchers and clinicians alike, yet little scientific inquiry has
been done in the AYA cancer population. Research in the usage of narratives in healthy
AYAs has established that expressive writing narratives have a positive impact on a number of areas (Travagin et al. 2015). The research done by Owenet al. (2005) has demonstrated that the expression of negative emotions may lead to better psychological adjustment. In one study of AYAs with cancer, it has been shown that that participating in
narrative writing can help teens with cancer discuss their physical complications, enhance
their feelings of control, and support the processing of thoughts and emotions (Kameny
and Bearison 2002). There is also research that highlights the benefits of verbal emotional
and self-disclosure, both in health and psychological domains (Laccetti 2007; Owen et al.
2006). Expressive writing has been shown to lower levels of distress and increase quality
of life in certain cancer populations (Frattaroli 2006; Stanton et al. 2000). Mellblom et al.
(2017) also found that the thematic content of narratives written by adolescent cancer survivors can be useful in predicting and evaluating their emotionality and the extent to which
the emotional and physical late effects of cancer treatment is effecting their daily lives.
Kuntz (2017) showed similar findings in current AYA cancer patients who recited cancer
narratives on a voice recorder.

Other Forms of Narrative Expressions as Intervention
Studies have shown that ruminative thoughts, without the act of expressive coping can
lead to higher levels of negative affect after a traumatic or stressful event (Horowitz 1993).
Written expressive disclosure improves physical and psychological health, especially when
the disclosure is related to a stressful life event (Frattaroli 2006). More specifically, Pennebaker and Francis (1996) identified a number of specific features about written narratives that could predict better health outcomes. This pilot study suggests that participants
who wrote about traumatic experiences (e.g. cancer and cancer treatment) with a higher
number of positive emotion words, a moderate number of negative emotion words, and an
increased use of cognitive words needed to see a physician significantly less than other participants. Positive associations with expressive writing have been related to the expression
and labelling of emotions surrounding the event (Lieberman et al. 2011), affirmation of
important domains of the self (Gilbert et al. 1998), and discovery of meaning related to the
negative event (Danoff-Burg et al. 2006). Therapeutic music and videos have also elicited
significantly more engaging behaviors and resilience in children in a hospital setting than
other therapeutic activities (Robb 2000; Robb et al. 2014). Written narratives (especially
when done online) have the addition benefit of allowing the writers time to think about the
content before deciding whether or not to share it with close others, allowing for further
processing of the information.
Without enough social interaction or support, adolescents with cancer may be physically isolated due to their illness or and socially isolated as they cope with their new identities as a young person with cancer. Websites such as Facebook, Twitter, and blog platforms
have become a common source for social and peer support amongst the AYA population
with and without cancer as they are able to share their stories and connect with peers who
understand what they going through (Treadgold and Kuperberg 2010). Online mediums
give AYAs with cancer a way to share narratives of their illness experience with true peers

who are dealing with similar health challenges and have the growing in support of the AYA
cancer community (Schiffman et al. 2008). There have been multiple studies that looked
at the significance of linguistic factors in online narrative. Owen et al. (2005) conducted
a randomized clinical trial of an online psychological support intervention and found that
expression of negative emotions was associated with better psychological adjustment.
Another form of narrative self-disclosure used by teens is the making of video testimonials, such as those found on websites like YouTube. Video testimonials may be used as a
method of self-monitoring and meaning making when adolescents are going through periods of stress or anxiety and recent studies have revealed that technological self-monitoring
has been effective in decreasing depressive symptoms (Kauer et al. 2012) and increasing
hope and resiliency in young adults following emotional trauma related to bereavement and
cancer (Mcgurl et al. 2015). Video therapy has been shown to lead to peer relationship resolution and general psychiatric improvement in adolescent girls with adjustment problems
(Gardano 1994), as well as having the added therapeutic benefit of more actively engaging
teens in their treatment (Furman 1990; Gardano 1994). While other research has seen positive gains working with young people who have experienced a number of traumas, including domestic violence, depression, and bereavement (Anderson and Wallace 2015; Johnson and Alderson 2008; McGurl et al. 2015). Technology-enhanced treatments (e.g. video
therapy) are not new as movies have been incorporated into therapy to increase insight for
a number of years (Wedding and Niemiec 2003). However, there is still limited research on
how the addition of technology in clinical practice will impact patients (Imel et al. 2017).
It may be possible that in the AYA population, the addition of technology will make the
therapeutic process more accessible, thereby increasing the benefits for this population.
Taking into consideration the increased use of technology in adolescent and young
adult populations, it shouldn't be surprising that technology- enhanced therapies have been
a subject of interest for researcher for decades now. As early as the late 1980's, Canter
(1989) found that computer-mediated art therapy was the most popular art therapy of teens
and young adults, largely due to this populations interaction with technology from a young
age. As technology has improved and become even more prominent, it wouldn't be surprising if these findings were even more significant for today's teens and young adults. For
teens with cancer, the use of technology in therapy may also be seen as a form of adaptive
technology. For children and teens with cancer, they are often living in hospitals or spending extensive amounts of time in outpatient facilities for treatment, limiting their accessibility to social support and peer-to-peer interaction. Adult breast cancer patients have used
online message boards to connect with one another, with research suggesting that posts
containing long and personal narratives elicit a higher level of support from peers (Lewallen et al. 2014). Technology could therefore be an effective way of allowing teens with a
chronic illness to reach out to one another, in circumstances where they may otherwise feel
both physically and emotionally isolated (Malchiodi and Johnson 2013).

The Role of Personality in Coping and Online Narrative Content
The way in which people cope with trauma and speak about their stressors may also be
impacted a great deal by their individual personality traits (Kushner 2015). In a study
examining the relationship between personality and physiological responses to emotional
video content, those high in Extraversion suggested less emotional distress experienced
when watching films with a negative valence while positive relationships with a number of physiological measures of stress were seen with the other Big 5 personality traits

(Brumbaugh et al. 2013). In adolescents with cancer, Neuroticism was found to be related
to negative interpretations when comparing one's cancer experience to other patients who
were doing poorly while comparisons to those doing better was positively related to Extroversion (Van der Zee et al. (1999). Neuroticism and Conscientiousness are also associated
with poorer coping in patients with cancer (Cardenal et al. 2012).
Building on previous research suggesting a connection between personality and language use in traditional written paradigms (Hirsh and Peterson 2009), researcher's desire
to understand people through social media use has led to research initiatives with the aim
of understanding people through their online behavior, such has inferring personality traits
through language use (Gill 2011). Some of the findings have only further solidified the connections between language use and self-presentation online. Ong et al. (2001), for example,
have found that participants who rated highly as extraverts more frequently posted verbal
status updates. Research focusing on even smaller chunks of verbal content online, such as
the "hashtag," is also revealing interesting findings about behavior and personal characteristics including establishing interpersonal relationships through identifying areas of common ground (Zappavigna 2015). However, research is lacking in this area in people with
chronic medical conditions.

Current Study
As part of a larger study, a number of personal characteristics of adolescents with cancer
were correlated with relevant linguistic content of their illness narratives. This was then
compared to the content of healthy adolescent peers' narratives discussing a recent significant stressor. Broadly, differences between the two groups across all factors explored were
expected. Based on previous analysis of this data (Keir et al. 2017; Pereira 2017; Pereira
et al. 2018; Sampson et al. 2017) and the pre-existing literature, the following hypotheses
were explored:

H1 Personality Factors will be related to emotional tone with Emotional Stability correlating negatively with negative talk and Extroversion correlating positively to positive talk.

H2 Knowledge of other testimonial videos will be related to thematic content, specially

participants with cancer will have more focus on health, present, and future events while
adolescents without cancer will have more focus on social topics and the future.

H3 Health Related Quality of Life will be related to verbal content. Better quality of life
will be positively correlated with positive language use while poorer quality of life will be
related to more social related language.

Participant Information
Two groups of participants were recruited for, adolescents diagnosed with cancer for the
first time and adolescence with no history of cancer. Each group could not have completed
high school prior to participation in the study and were between the ages of 13-18 during
filming. Recruitment took place at a number of sites of which IRB and/or organizational
research committee approval was given (Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, Children's
Hospital of Orange County, and Maria Fareri Children's Hospital) and via advertisements
placed online or places of high traffic to desired populations (schools, locations of extracurricular activities, etc.). A total of N = 25 participants (see Table 1) were recruited but
final analyses only included 20 participants (see Pereira 2017 for additional details).

N = 10 participants were between the ages of 12 and 17 when diagnosed with cancer. The
sample consisted of 4 males (1 Caucasian, 2 Hispanic, and 1 Asian) and 6 females from
different demographic backgrounds (1 mixed, 2 Caucasian, and 2 Hispanic). The average

Control Population (Healthy Adolescents)
Participants in the final group (n = 10) of healthy controls were matched by gender and
were between 13 and 17 years old during filming. The overall sample (n = 14) consisted of
5 males (1 Hispanic, 3 Caucasian, and 1 Asian) and 9 females (1 Asian, 6 Caucasian, and 2
mixed) and spanned grades 8 through 12.

Measures
Demographics
Demographic items were developed to assess for a participant's health history (cancer
patients answered additional items around their cancer experience), social media usage,
and other information about each group. Knowledge about typical video testimonials
presented on social media and comfort in communicating about one's feelings were also
assessed via items on this questionnaire. Slightly different versions of the questionnaire
existed for each population as many of the illness based items on Target version were not
relevant to the control group.

Quality of Life
Version 4.0 of the Pediatric Quality of Life-Core Items (PedsQL) was used to assess participant health related quality of life over the past month. The PedsQL measures physical,
emotional, social, and school functioning based upon the patients age (Varni et al. 2002).
This measure was initially developed using pediatric cancer patients and has been shown to
be a reliable and valid measure in both this population as well as healthy patients without
a significant medical history (Varni et al. 1999, 2002). It should be noted that each item
is usually reverse scored with higher scores indicating better quality of life. This was not
done during final analyses and thus higher scores indicate worse quality of life.

Personality
The Ten Item Personality (TIPI) (Gosling et al. 2003) was used to quickly examine aspects
of the big five personality traits and what their relationship could be to aspects of disclosure and benefits experienced from making the film. The researchers selected this measure
because it has been found to have adequate levels of test-retest reliability as well as, discriminant and convergent validity without placing significant burden on the participants
(Gosling et al. 2003).

Linguistic Analysis
The computer program, Linguistic Inquiry Word Count (LIWC) (Pennebaker et al. 2015),
was chosen to analyze linguistic content. A number of studies have shown LIWC to be a
valid instrument with respect to measuring variables relevant to the study population (e.g.
emotional expression, illness history, etc.) and has also been widely used in cancer populations (Bantum and Owen 2009; Crook et al. 2016; Laccetti 2007; Liess et al. 2008; Pasupathi 2007).

Impact of Filming
A self-report questionnaire, the Post Video Impressions Questionnaire (PVIQ), was developed for the purpose of this study to measure beliefs about the filming process following
the completion of making one's video narrative. The questions were rated on a scale of 1
(strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Questions such as, "After making this video I felt
sad," "Others watching this video would feel something positive," and "Making this video
changed the way I think about my experiences with cancer" were included. Items were
also slightly different in each version of this questionnaire (e.g. "my illness" or "my cancer" was replaced with "my hardship" etc. on the control version). The PVIQ was included
in this study to further explore the potential impacts seen from illness narratives. Internal
reliability of the PVIQ were ran in an earlier publication of this research and found to be
strong for both the target (.935) and control (.905) populations (Pereira 2017).

Procedure
Testing and Analyses
Full testing procedures can be reviewed in the dissertation of the first author on this paper
(Pereira 2017). For the purposes of this paper, after video narratives were transcribed they
analyzed using LIWC to examine the linguistic content (see Table 2 for relevant codes). As
previously mentioned, research suggests that linguistic content of a narrative can provides
information about the health and well-being of the narrator (Chung and Pennebaker 2007).
Variables were chosen for analysis to examine both their relationships to each other and
how they differ between groups. In deciding which variables to consider, verbal content
factors were chosen based on their general nature and relevance as common topics discussed in other AYA narrative literature (Drew 2003; Grinyer 2007; Kameny and Bearison 2002; Pereira et al. 2017). They included areas of HRQOL (biological and emotional
experiences and social relationships), processing of the event, and focus of time. Two sets
of analyses were conducted focusing on the relationship of these variables with personality
traits of participants and other key characteristics of participants.

Time
A number of personality traits were negatively correlated with focus on different time
points in one's cancer journey. Extraversion was significantly negatively correlated with
future talk while Emotional Stability (i.e. Neuroticism) and Openness were trending in this
direction as well. A significant negative relationship was also found between Emotional
Stability and language focused on the past. Openness had a strong but not significant relationship in this area while a correlation between Conscientiousness and Present focused
language also revealed a strong negative correlation. Meanwhile, in healthy adolescents
these relationships showed more consistent patterns between personality traits. Patients
high in extroversion, agreeableness, and openness (to a lesser degree) were found to have
more past focused language and less present focused language. Patients high in conscientiousness found an opposite relationship between these two time stamps. Emotional stability was the only personality factor found to have a strong correlation with future-talk
(r = - .406), although it was not significant.
Participant characteristics also revealed additional relationships with time focused language. Knowledge about video testimonials showed strong relationships with each tense.
In adolescents with cancer, positive relationships were found with past and present tense
usage and a negative relationship with future talk. Comfort in discussing one's feelings
suggested a significant positive relationship with present focused language in this group
as well. All of these relationships were the opposite in healthy teens. Poor HRQOL was
related to past and future talk in adolescents with cancer. While this relationship was only
found in present focused language in healthy adolescents.

Quality of Life Areas
Affect
Participants in both groups who were high in extroversion typically used less positive emotional language and more negative emotional language. Participants in both groups who
were high in conscientiousness also tended to used more affective language, and positive
language in general. Healthy adolescents high in conscientiousness were less likely to
use negative emotional language, which is congruent with previous research suggesting
that higher level of conscientiousness are associated with lower levels of negative affect
(Javaras et al. 2012). Agreeableness in adolescents with cancer and openness in healthy
adolescents were both associated with less negative emotional language. Interestingly, no
relationships were seen between Emotional Stability and participants in either group.

All other participant factors were related to negative relationships in all affective language usage in adolescents with cancer. Correlations looking at comfort in communicating

Biological Experiences
As would be expected (primarily as a result of the highly physical nature of the topic of the
film), personality traits in adolescents with cancer were all associated with high biological
and body focused language. Openness, however, was the only significant relationship found
(r = .569, p < .05). Meanwhile, all other participant factors explored show no relationship.
Positive relationships between Extraversion, Agreeableness, Openness, knowledge about
videos, and comfort in communicating about one's feelings were found in health adolescents. No significant differences between groups on any correlation value were seen.

Social Relationships
With respect to language focused on social relationships, both groups of adolescents
showed similar patterns of language if they were high on extraversion (positive) and conscientiousness (negative), although these were stronger in adolescents with cancer. Emotional stability was also found to have a positive trend in adolescents with cancer. Knowledge about films and comfort in communicating feelings in adolescents with cancer both
showed positive relationships with social focused language while HRQOL was found to
have a negative relationship. In healthy adolescents the relationship found in HRQOL was
positive. This relationship was found to be significantly different (z = 1.75, p < .05).

Discussion
Results of this study continues to expand the literature around linguistic content of illness narratives and factors that may be related to this. While the hypotheses were not fully
supported, a number of interesting differences were highlighted between each group that
suggest variations in each group, despite noted benefits that they both experience (Pereira
2017). Limitations are shared below within the context of the results and directions for
future research.
Adolescents with cancer appeared to use more processing based language when they had
worse quality of life (QOL), while the opposite was true for healthy peers. In earlier analysis
of this data, it was found that healthy adolescents also used more social based language versus
personal focused language. Based on items related to the impact of filming, it was suggested
that these results were due to an increased desire for others to understand them as opposed to
need to understand one's self (while the opposite appeared true for adolescents with cancer
(Pereira 2017). The findings of this paper appear to support that hypothesis. Additional findings may also suggest that, based on personality type and emotional language used, adolescents with cancer appeared to avoid discussing topics in a negative way. While this may be a

13

Journal of Psycholinguistic Research (2019) 48:1185-1201

1197

result of coping skills related to meaning making or personal grit, other literature suggests this
may be due to avoidance of causing distress in others as a result of their own personal suffering (Warner et al. 2016). However, other research looking into the responses to video testimonials posted by young people with eating disorders suggests that viewers typically have positive responses to these stories (Pereira et al. 2016). When considering the findings by Cardenal
et al. (2012) and the host of literature around the importance of peer support (Kent et al. 2013;
Tillery et al. 2017), the disconnect between patient's perceptions of others responding to their
stories versus the reality may be an area of clinical concern for those working with adolescents
with cancer. Given the successes seen in group film creation, incorporating the help of peers
to complete videos may a way to work around this struggle as well as improve overall peer
relationships (Gardano 1994).
Of importance is the difference in social quality of life and its relation to a number of
linguistic factors. In adolescents with cancer, worse social QOL was related to using social
focused language more, with the opposite being true for adolescents with no history of cancer.
It is a generally accepted construct that social quality of life and social relationships are significantly important for all adolescents, but this is especially true for adolescents with cancer.
Some of the most effective methods of psychological intervention include a social component to them and social support is both highly desired by AYA cancer patients and related to
increased post-traumatic growth (D'Agostino et al. 2011; Warner et al. 2016; Yi et al. 2015).
Social Quality of life was found to be a strong factor across a number of other thematic components of the narratives (Keir et al. 2017), continuing to support the importance of social
functioning and thus quality of life with AYA patients with cancer.
The two biggest limitations in this study were the time in which participants were asked
to complete their films and that healthy controls were asked to speak about a difficult experience instead of a specific type of different experience (leading to possible greater variability in
language usage). Healthy controls completed their narrative at differing time points. In some
cases participants were still actively going through their stressor while in others their stressor
had taken place months or over a year ago while adolescents were cancer were all currently in
treatment. This, in turn, influences the tense of the films and may also influence one's ability
to tell a straightforward and cohesive narrative. In adolescents with cancer, those with worse
quality of life spent more time talking about the past or future suggesting that talking about the
present was too stressful. This relationship was not seen in healthy peers.
Impact of the event on one's life was measured differently in each population and thus
could not be effectively compared. A similar method of measuring this variable could have led
to interesting comparisons around severity, time since event (which was not asked of healthy
controls), and the impact on tense within the narrative. Especially as differences in processing related language may highlight both how these two groups process difficult and traumatic
events as well as what their potential psychological needs may be as a result. Research suggests that adolescents with cancer who receive any form of peer support (including from
healthy teens), have experienced benefits (Tillery et al. 2017). The current research may suggest that there are benefits that are not understood yet that true peers in each group may have
that others do not.

Conclusion
Despite the pilot nature of this research and small sample size, results are still suggestive
of interesting relationships (especially as they relate to social relationships) around relationships of linguistic content of video narratives of adolescents with and without cancer.
Additional research with a larger population is warranted to better understand factors influencing content and outcomes. Continuing this research may lead to eventual development
of video narrative based interventions that can help young people with cancer or other significant stressors cope in a way that in both interesting and useful to the new generation of
patients.
