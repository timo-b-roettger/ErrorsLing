Prevalence of Stuttering in African
American Preschoolers
Adele Proctor
Ehud Yairi
University of Illinois at Urbana - Champaign

Melissa C. Duff
University of Iowa

Jie Zhang
University of Illinois at Urbana - Champaign

Purpose: In this study, the authors sought to determine the prevalence of stuttering
in African American (AA) 2- to 5-year-olds as compared with same-age European
Americans (EAs).
Method: A total of 3,164 children participated: 2,223 AAs and 941 EAs. Data
were collected using a 3-pronged approach that included investigators' individual
interactions with each child, teacher identification, and parent identification of
stuttering.
Results: No statistically significant difference for stuttering was found between AA and
EA children. Using the investigator and teacher method of identification, the
prevalence of stuttering was 2.52% for the entire sample. For both racial groups, boys
exhibited a higher prevalence of stuttering than girls. Of the 3 predictors (age, race,
sex) of stuttering, only sex was a significant predictor.
Conclusions: AA 2- to 5-year-olds are not overrepresented in the stuttering
population for this age group. When data are combined for both racial groups, the
prevalence of stuttering is 2.52%. More boys than girls stuttered in this sample of
preschoolers.

relative contribution of each type of communication disorder to this
phenomenon remains unclear. One approach to clarify this issue is to
systematically conduct disorder-specific epidemiological studies.

As a first step in determining which communication disorders, if any, reveal disproportionate numbers
of African Americans with speech and language impairment, the present investigation focused only on stuttering in 2- to 5-year olds. In several important respects,
stuttering is a disorder of early childhood, as in the majority of cases because stuttering begins and stops between 20 and 60 months of age. It is estimated that 75%
to 90% of all children experience stuttering onset before
age 5 (Andrews, 1984; Yairi & Ambrose, 1999). Yet, as
noted by Yairi and Ambrose (2005), there have been fewer
studies on the occurrence and distribution of stuttering in
preschoolers than in school-age children or adults. Consequently, research-based information on the incidence and
prevalence of stuttering among preschool-age children is
relatively sparse. Additionally, some past studies invite
questions concerning methodological issues, such as the
sole reliance on the identification of stuttering by health
workers' home visits, which, at times, relied on parents'
reports, not their own direct observations (Andrews &
Harris, 1964); the sole reliance on parental retrospective
reports (e.g., Glasner & Rosenthal, 1957); or on clinical
files (Dalston, Martinkosky, & Hinton, 1987). More recently, Craig, Hancock, Tran, Craig, and Peters (2002)
used a telephone interview with a household member to
identify stuttering, including young children. When possible, they also obtained a 5-min speech sample that was
recorded over the telephone.
There are several reasons for obtaining more accurate prevalence data on stuttering among AA preschool children. First, there are increasing numbers of
AA preschoolers in the U.S. population (McKinnon, 2003;
U.S. Census Bureau, 2002). Second, among speechlanguage pathologists (SLPs), there has been a general
belief that there is a higher frequency of occurrence of
stuttering in AA children than in EA children (Cooper &
Cooper, 1998; Dean & Brown, 1977; Gillespie & Cooper,
1973). If SLPs in clinical positions perceive that more
AA children stutter, this could lead to a self-fulfilling
prophecy of identifying stuttering where one expects to
find stuttering. Finally, the completion of a series of
epidemiologic studies may provide data distinguishing
which communication disorders are the strongest contributors to the overrepresentation of African Americans
in speech and language impairment. Inasmuch as no

study has explored the incidence or prevalence of stuttering in AA preschool children, the age during which
the disorder typically begins, the purpose of the present
investigation was to determine the prevalence of stuttering in AA preschool children.
After reviewing many surveys of stuttering in
American schoolchildren, Bloodstein (1995) estimated
that the stuttering incidence ranged from a low of 0.70%
to a high of 15.40% and reported that the prevalence
of stuttering ranged from 0.35% to 2.12%. Andrews and
Harris (1964) followed British English-speaking children
from birth to age 16 years and reported that the incidence
was 4.9%, whereas the prevalence of stuttering was 1%.
Mansson (2000) followed Danish preschoolers over a period of several years and reported a stuttering incidence
of 5.19%. Craig et al. (2002) reported a prevalence of
1.4% for children 2-5 years of age in a household sample
of the population of New South Wales, Australia. In
Belgium, Van Borsel et al. (2006) found a prevalence rate
of 0.58% for all 6- to 20-year-olds who attended regular
schools. The highest prevalence (0.78%), however, was
detected in the youngest group of 6- to-10-year-olds. A
recent survey of more than 10,000 primary school students in Australia, based on teacher identification and
confirmed by SLPs' records, yielded a very low stuttering prevalence of 0.33% (McKinnon, McLeod, & Reilly,
2007).
Overall, only a handful of studies of incidence and
prevalence of stuttering in preschool children have been
conducted, and those that are published are not representative of the culturally and linguistically diverse
populations in the United States. Thus, the strength of
inferences and the generalizations of past findings may
have questionable applicability for African Americans
and other minorities in the United States. Because there
have been many suggestions that race and /or ethnicity
influence the occurrence of stuttering, particularly among
African Americans (Cooper & Cooper, 1998; Dean &
Brown, 1977; Gillespie & Cooper, 1973), the importance
of obtaining epidemiological data for African Americans
and other U.S. minorities becomes even more apparent.
The central tendency of the various incidence estimates indicates that about 5% of the population has
ever stuttered, whereas the prevalence is much lower--
that is, only 1% or less stutter at a given time. By
inference, the discrepancy between the figures for incidence and prevalence suggests that the disorder persists
and becomes chronic in only 20% of all cases. Or, conversely, there appears to be a high rate of recovery
(Andrews & Harris, 1964; Mansson, 2000; Yairi &
Ambrose, 2005). Previous studies of incidence and prevalence of stuttering in African Americans are scarce,
findings are conflicting and confusing, and no studies
have examined AA preschool children. A critical literature
search was completed to ascertain studies on stuttering

the studies in Table 1 reported how race was determined

relations and family organization--may provide contexts
for interpreting data. Goldman (1967) explored stuttering in AA children relative to different types of family organizations, an important sociocultural variable.

patriarchal families, operational definitions were not

European Americans, but the number of children participating in the study was not specified. Therefore,
without information on the sample size, no prevalence

Significantly, from 1916 to 1967, investigations on
AA children were conducted in racially segregated school
systems, a fact that has implications for overlooking the
possible effects of African American English (AAE) on
the findings. In these early investigations, it is probable
that the children spoke AAE, a language variety (dialect)
spoken by a large number of AA children, adolescents,
and adults (Dillard, 1973; Labov, 1972). It is a rulegoverned linguistic system in which different types of
normal disfluencies represent distinct semantic functions
(Green, 2002; Smitherman, 1977). For example, more
typically normal disfluencies, such as revisions, phrase
repetitions, filled pauses, and prosodic features, denote
various meanings in AAE (Anderson, 1981; Asante, 1990;
Mufwene, 1993; Mufwene, Rickford, Bailey, & Baugh,
1998; Poplack, 2000; Rickford, 1999). Observing the presence of a combination of some or all of these linguistic
features in a speaker may be mistaken for stuttering
by individuals who are not familiar with AAE (Proctor,
2006).
Insufficient experience in recognizing language
variation may have affected the diagnosis of stuttering
in studies limited to AA children as well as in studies
of bilingualism that also included AA children. For instance, in examining the relationship between bilingualism and stuttering, Travis et al. (1937) did not
specify language data of the participant population. It
appears that the differentiation of the presence or absence of stuttering did not consider normal disfluencies
that may be associated with second-language learning
(Dale, 1977; Karniol, 1992; Nwokah, 1988; Watson &
Kayser, 1994), nor did it consider the range of normal
disfluencies embedded in the AAE linguistic rule system. In any study of communication disorders, how one
defines stuttering, how practitioners apply definitions
of stuttering, and how questions are phrased when cultural informants are involved require specification. Further, the investigators' knowledge of the language and
culture of the target population are among variables

Maes, & Foulon, 2001; Zimmerman, Liljeblad, Frank, &

Background Data and Staff Qualifications

To achieve our objective, the target population consisted of 2- to 5-year-old African Americans and sameaged European Americans who were attending regular
programs for the general population in their respective areas in the state of Illinois. The sample was drawn
from 49 different, racially integrated preschools in urban, rural, small-town, and suburban areas in Illinois,
reflecting a broad stratum of communities with different
levels of economic resources. Preschools included both
private and public programs, and economic resources varied among individual children within the same schools.
Geographically, populations from the north, south, east,
west, and central areas of Illinois were targeted.

The present investigation addressed several variables that are crucial to research in diverse cultural and
linguistic groups and to the identification of communication disorders in these groups. Most important, each
of the 3,164 participant children received individual
speech, language, voice, and fluency screening. A measure that is sensitive to characteristics of AAE, the
Fluharty Preschool Speech and Language Screening Test
(Fluharty, 1978), was used for the speech and language
portions of the screening. For 2,151 of the participants,
the preschool SLPs also provided speech-language-voice
screening data based on the American Speech-LanguageHearing Association (ASHA, 2004) practice protocols,
which they routinely administered. Therefore, the 1,013
children for whom these data were not available were
administered the ASHA practice protocols by the three
certified SLPs who collected data for this research project. A hearing screening was administered to all children, and only those who passed were included.

Participants

cases, regional and social dialects co-occurred in the

General Strategy and Definition
To reduce risks of errors in relying on a single source
of stuttering identification, a three-pronged data collection approach was conducted. The three-pronged approach
consisted of (a) the investigator's face-to-face interaction
with each child, (b) teacher reports on each child, and
(c) parent reports on each of their children. The total
number of participating children was determined by the
number of families that signed consent forms agreeing to
their child's participation in the survey conducted at the
respective school. Stuttering was operationally defined
as an attention-calling occurrence of sound, syllable, and
single-syllable word repetitions, sound prolongations, or
complete blockages of speech. These could be associated
with facial grimaces, eye blinks, head movements, and/or
other body movements and struggles. Significantly, each
child who was identified as exhibiting stuttering, based
on the operational definition of stuttering, was so diagnosed by either two SLPs who observed his or her speaking
or by a teacher followed by the examination of two SLPs.

Data Collection
The first author telephoned directors of preschools
throughout the state of Illinois identified from published
lists provided by local, state, and federal agencies. The
project's objectives were explained, and permission was
requested to individually observe and interact with children, interview teachers, and contact parents. After permission was granted, a packet of printed materials that
included an explanatory letter, a brief parent questionnaire (see Appendix), and a statement of informed consent was distributed to parents through the collaborating
schools. After the preschools received returned consent
forms and questionnaires from the families, visits to the
schools were scheduled.
Teacher identification. A total of 220 classroom teachers from 49 schools participated. Of the children with
signed parental consent forms, the primary classroom
teacher was asked to indicate on the classroom roster
those suspected of, or identified as, having any type of
speech (including articulation and stuttering), language,
voice, or hearing difficulty. Verbal/vocal examples of specific features of these communication disorders were given.

Investigators' screening. For each preschool visit,
there was one SLP/investigator in charge of the testing

Investigators individually observed each child with

speech-language problem, including fluency, the second investigator was asked to re-examine the child and

teacher had a concern about the student. Thus, in all

Children who were identified as stuttering by any data
source were audiotaped, and tapes were further examined to determine presence or absence of stuttering.
Parent identification. The families of each of the
children received two documents: (a) a letter concerning
the study with an attached consent form for the child's

voice, and fluency problems. Parents were invited to
return the consent form and the questionnaire. Face-toface counseling pertaining to parents' concerns was offered, but only a few took advantage of the offer. The
investigator did not examine returned parental questionnaires prior to the beginning of the screenings.

Results
Race Comparisons
Investigators' procedures for identification of stuttering were applied to the entire sample of 3,164 preschoolers. With this method, 81 children (2.56%) were
identified as exhibiting stuttering. Of the 2,223 AA subset,
58 (2.60%) stuttered, and of the 941 EA subset, 23 (2.44%)
stuttered. A c2 test indicated no significant difference
between the two racial groups, c2(1, N = 3,164) = 0.07,
p > .05.

pertinent to briefly compare its central methodological
features with those of previous investigations of stuttering prevalence among African Americans. First, the age
of the participants is important. Whereas all previous
studies examined African Americans of elementary

much more information is gained about occurrence and
distribution of stuttering during the early years of life
rather than in any other period. When investigating
children who are only 3 or 4 years older than the current
sample, we found that approximately 75%, if not more, of
those who previously were diagnosed as stuttering have
already experienced the disappearance of their stuttering due to natural recovery (Andrews, 1984; Andrews &
Harris, 1964; Mansson, 2000; Yairi & Ambrose, 2005).
This factor is particularly strong for girls, a fact that
clouds the true sex ratio in stuttering, which, owing to
recovery, greatly increases with age. Second, previous
studies used only a single source of information, primarily
teachers' responses to questionnaires. In a few studies, a
single investigator or a school clinician identified children
who stuttered. In the present study, investigators, teachers, and parents served as the three sources of identification (although one provided only partial data). Further,
all children who stuttered were so identified by at least
two people making an independent judgment based on
direct observations. Also, the present study provided a
clear definition of stuttering, and all children who were
suspected of stuttering were audio tape-recorded for additional verification. Last, in the present study, the
investigators /interviewers were knowledgeable about
the AA culture and English language used by African
Americans and, therefore, were better able to interact
with the children and to distinguish normal disfluencies from stuttering.
In light of the above, key findings from this study
provide noteworthy information about stuttering in
AA preschoolers. Most pertinent to the objectives of
the current study is that all three data sources indicated
no statistically significant differences in the prevalence
of stuttering for 2- to 5-year-old African Americans and
same-age European Americans during the period when
the overwhelming majority of stuttering onsets occur.
The similarity between the two racial groups extended
to factors of age and sex. In both racial groups, boys
exhibited a higher prevalence of stuttering than girls
in ratios that are similar to what has been previously
reported, thus adding validity to the findings, and there
were no statistically significant differences in the overall prevalence of stuttering among the four discrete age
groups within the sample. Also, both groups exhibited
similar patterns of changing sex ratio with age. Investigator and teacher identification of stuttering revealed
that the prevalence of stuttering was 2.52% for this
sample of preschoolers. Still, although our large sample
was very diverse, it should be noted that the children
lived in a single state in the United States. Thus, inasmuch as the present study is the first to examine the
prevalence of stuttering in AA preschool children with

For example, the present study did not rely on children
being brought to a laboratory setting for diagnosis. Instead,
children were identified during interaction in naturalistic contexts.

The present study elicited stuttering identification,
as well as additional information, from parents, but only
1,107 (35%) of them responded. Although the reason for
this medium level of responding to a questionnaire survey is not clear, it is interesting that, contrary to previous reports (e.g., Yairi & Ambrose, 2005), parents tended
to identify children as currently exhibiting stuttering,
whereas the investigators and teachers did not. Four
reasons may account for this phenomenon: (a) parents'
confusion between past and present stuttering, (b) children might stutter at home but not in school or vice versa,
(c) parents may have identified some other type of speech
disorder as stuttering, and (d) certain types of nonverbal
behaviors may have been considered as stuttering. It is
possible that the minority of parents who responded
represent those who were particularly sensitive about
their children's speech, a possibility that is a factor in
the previous explanations. These same reasons may account for some of the investigator and teacher mismatches.
Regardless, the differences between parent identification of stuttering and investigator and teacher identification invite further investigation regarding factors
that influence parent perception and assessment of
stuttering.

In summary, the results of the present study contribute to dispelling the long-held belief among many
practitioners and researchers that a higher percentage
of African Americans stutter than do European Americans. Still, the current study did not investigate the
possible racial or ethnic factors in natural recovery. The
possibility that African Americans exhibit a smaller recovery rate, and hence a higher percentage of stuttering
prevalence in later ages, should also be investigated. The
results of this study also support the need for culturally and linguistically sensitive research in stuttering--
research that addresses a variety of issues such as the
prevalence of stuttering in other racial/ethnic groups,
older age groups, alternative research designs, and treatment efficacy in diverse populations.

As the number of culturally and linguistically diverse groups increase in the United States, there is a
commensurate demand for strong, empirically generated
theoretical bases to support assessment and intervention
practices. A systematic approach to developing a scientific body of information prior to sorting out what types of
assessment and treatment processes are appropriate for
AA preschool children should begin with establishing
epidemiological data such as the prevalence of stuttering. Data from the present investigation suggest that
investigators may not expect to observe more AA 2- to
5-year-olds who stutter than EA peers in this age group.
The current research base in early childhood stuttering
in the United States has examined stuttering in the context of mainstream American English-speaking children
and has not considered the diversity of the life experiences of children from racially/ethnically and linguistically diverse backgrounds. As a result of such a view of
children who stutter, there remains a lack of knowledge
of treatment efficacy for children from AA culture and
from other diverse groups residing in the United States.
Some investigators (Kathard, 2001; Mower, 1998) argued
that the experimental designs in stuttering have not accommodated the complexity of stuttering as a social issue.
Consequently, creative and alternative strategies are required to expand the existing view about how investigations of stuttering should be carried out (Perkins, 2000).
